# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Context and Motivation

Corporate meetings at multinational organisations such as Nestlé routinely bring together speakers of different first languages, accents, and levels of fluency, often within a single conversation that drifts between English, French, German, and other languages without warning. This linguistic variability, combined with the acoustic reality of open-plan offices, video-conferencing hardware, and overlapping speech, places considerable strain on any system that tries to produce a written record of what was said. For most attendees this is an inconvenience; for employees with hearing impairments, an accurate, low-latency transcript is frequently the only practical way to participate in a meeting at all. Automatic speech recognition (ASR) is the natural technology to close this gap, but the same conditions that make corporate meetings hard for human listeners, noise, code-switching, and domain-specific vocabulary, are precisely the conditions under which general-purpose ASR systems are known to degrade (Radford et al., 2023; Baevski et al., 2020).

This thesis was developed in the context of a Nestlé/BIT collaboration exploring whether an ASR pipeline built from openly available models could be deployed, evaluated, and iteratively improved for this use case without relying on a proprietary, closed-box transcription service. The guiding motivation is twofold: first, to determine how far a freely available model such as OpenAI's Whisper, run through the CTranslate2-optimised faster-whisper implementation (SYSTRAN, 2023), can be pushed using only open preprocessing and prompting techniques; and second, to do so honestly, by measuring rather than assuming the resulting accuracy, speed, and failure modes. Early, informal experimentation during the prototyping phase of this project surfaced a recurring class of error, plausible-sounding but wrong transcriptions of proper names and domain terms, that motivated much of the experimental design described in later chapters. Rather than rely on that anecdotal impression, this thesis builds a small but methodologically sound evaluation pipeline and reports what it actually found.

## 1.2 Problem Statement

The central problem addressed in this thesis is the following: general-purpose, multilingual ASR models perform measurably worse in noisy, code-switched, and domain-specific corporate settings than the headline benchmarks reported in their original publications would suggest, and the magnitude of that degradation, along with the effectiveness of common mitigation strategies, is rarely quantified for the specific combination of conditions a given organisation will face. Vendors and open-source maintainers report word error rate (WER) figures on curated, mostly clean, mostly monolingual benchmark sets; an organisation deciding whether to deploy ASR for accessibility purposes has comparatively little evidence about how those numbers translate to its own meetings. Three sub-problems follow directly from this: (1) how much does background noise actually degrade transcription accuracy, and can lightweight, open preprocessing recover some of that loss; (2) does injecting organisation-specific vocabulary into the model's decoding process measurably reduce errors on names and domain terms, given that this is the most commonly proposed low-cost mitigation; and (3) can automatic language identification be trusted in a genuinely multilingual meeting, or does it introduce errors of its own. This thesis treats all three as empirical questions to be measured, not assumed.

## 1.3 Research Objectives

The work pursues four objectives. First, to assemble a small but real, ground-truth-labelled, multilingual audio dataset suitable for measuring ASR performance under both clean and noisy conditions, given that no existing Nestlé-specific corpus was available within the scope and constraints of this thesis. Second, to implement and document an ASR pipeline based on faster-whisper that supports configurable preprocessing, domain-vocabulary prompting, and both automatic and forced language identification, while being explicit about which components of the broader architecture (such as voice activity detection) are fully wired into the transcription path and which remain partially implemented. Third, to run a controlled, multi-condition evaluation of that pipeline and report word error rate (WER), character error rate (CER), and real-time factor (RTF) without adjusting the experimental design after seeing unfavourable results. Fourth, to interpret those results honestly in light of the thesis's original research questions, including where the evidence is inconclusive or contradicts initial expectations.

## 1.4 Research Questions

This thesis is organised around four research questions, each addressed empirically in Chapter 6:

RQ1. What is the effect of lightweight noise-reduction preprocessing on WER, separately for clean and artificially noised audio, when using a compact Whisper model on CPU-only hardware?

RQ2. Does injecting a static, domain-specific vocabulary prompt into the decoder measurably reduce transcription errors, and if the effect is small or absent, what does that imply about when prompt-based domain adaptation is and is not useful?

RQ3. How reliable is Whisper's built-in automatic language identification across English, French, and German speech, and what are the practical consequences of a wrong language guess?

RQ4. What is the real-time factor of a CPU-only faster-whisper deployment at different model sizes, and what accuracy/speed trade-off does moving from a smaller to a larger model size actually buy in this setting?

## 1.5 Contributions of This Work

This thesis makes four contributions. First, it documents the construction of a small, licensed, multilingual evaluation set (English, French, German; clean and synthetically noised) built from the PolyAI/MInDS-14 corpus (Gerz et al., 2021) after several originally planned alternatives proved impractical within the project's sandboxed, offline-leaning environment; the rationale and trade-offs of that substitution are reported rather than concealed. Second, it reports a 180-run pilot evaluation across five experimental conditions, with WER, CER, and RTF computed using a self-contained, dependency-light implementation rather than an opaque third-party scoring library. Third, it identifies and explicitly reports two implementation gaps discovered during evaluation, namely that the voice-activity-detection component is defined in the codebase but never invoked by the transcription path, and that the language actually detected per utterance was not persisted in the evaluation logs, both of which shaped what could and could not be concluded from the data. Fourth, it replaces the anecdotal, frequently-repeated performance claims found in earlier drafts of this project's documentation with measured figures, including cases where the data did not support the originally expected hypothesis.

## 1.6 Thesis Structure

Chapter 2 introduces the technical vocabulary and evaluation metrics used throughout the thesis, alongside the characteristics of corporate meetings that motivate the work. Chapter 3 reviews the literature on ASR architectures, noise robustness, domain adaptation, and speaker diarisation relevant to this setting. Chapter 4 describes the system architecture that was actually implemented, including the points where the implementation diverges from the originally planned design. Chapter 5 details the dataset, experimental conditions, and evaluation methodology used in the pilot study. Chapter 6 presents the results of that study and interprets them against the research questions in Section 1.4. Chapter 7 discusses integration into a broader OCR/RAG-based meeting-intelligence platform and the security and deployment considerations that follow. Chapter 8 concludes with a summary of findings, an honest account of limitations, and directions for future work.

# 2. Background and Foundational Concepts

## 2.1 Automatic Speech Recognition: Core Concepts

Automatic speech recognition converts an acoustic waveform into text. Classical systems decomposed this task into separate, independently trained stages: a feature-extraction front end (commonly producing mel-frequency cepstral coefficients), an acoustic model estimating the probability of phonetic units given those features (historically a Gaussian Mixture Model over Hidden Markov Model states, GMM-HMM), a pronunciation lexicon, and a separately trained statistical language model, all combined during a search-based decoding step (Li, Deng, Gong, and Haeb-Umbach, 2014). Over the past decade this pipeline has been progressively replaced by end-to-end neural architectures that learn the mapping from audio to text directly, using connectionist temporal classification (Graves, 2012), attention-based encoder-decoders (Bahdanau et al., 2016), or, in the case of Whisper, a sequence-to-sequence transformer trained with simple multitask supervision on a very large and weakly labelled corpus of audio paired with transcripts (Radford et al., 2023). The distinction between CTC and attention-based decoding matters for what kind of errors each architecture tends to make: CTC assumes conditional independence between output positions given the input, which makes it fast to decode but weak at modelling fluent, grammatical dependencies between words, whereas an attention-based encoder-decoder, including Whisper's decoder, explicitly conditions each output token on all previously generated tokens, which produces more fluent output but can also make it more prone to confidently continuing a plausible-sounding sentence that has drifted away from what was actually said, a failure mode commonly referred to as hallucination and one that recurs in the qualitative error analysis in Chapter 6. A second major family, exemplified by wav2vec 2.0, instead learns a general-purpose audio representation through self-supervised pretraining on unlabelled audio before a comparatively small amount of labelled fine-tuning (Baevski et al., 2020). Both families are relevant to this thesis: Whisper-derived models are the ones actually evaluated in Chapters 5 and 6, while wav2vec 2.0 and related self-supervised approaches are discussed in Chapter 3 as an alternative architecture this project considered but did not, in the end, deploy in parallel.

The system implemented for this thesis uses faster-whisper, a CTranslate2-based reimplementation of Whisper's inference graph that supports INT8 quantisation on CPU (SYSTRAN, 2023). Quantisation reduces the numerical precision of model weights and activations, trading a small amount of accuracy for substantially lower memory use and faster inference, which matters directly for a system intended to run without dedicated GPU hardware. Whisper additionally accepts an "initial prompt", a short piece of text prepended to the decoder's context that can bias subsequent transcription toward particular spellings or vocabulary; this mechanism is the basis for the domain-vocabulary-prompting condition evaluated in Chapter 6.

Whisper is released as a family of checkpoints of increasing size, each trading inference cost for accuracy: `tiny` (39 million parameters), `base` (74 million), `small` (244 million), `medium` (769 million), and `large` (1.55 billion), with each successive size reported by its original authors to further reduce WER on their benchmark suite at the cost of higher memory use and slower inference (Radford et al., 2023). This thesis works exclusively with the two smallest checkpoints, `tiny` and `base`. This is a choice forced by the CPU-only, no-GPU sandbox environment in which all development and evaluation for this thesis took place (detailed in Section 4.5), not a claim that these are the most accurate sizes available; the resulting accuracy/speed trade-off between them is measured directly, rather than assumed, in Section 6.5.

## 2.2 Evaluation Metrics for ASR Systems

Three metrics are used throughout this thesis. Word Error Rate (WER) is the edit distance between a hypothesis transcript and a reference transcript, computed at the word level as the sum of substitutions, deletions, and insertions divided by the number of words in the reference, and is the de facto standard metric for ASR accuracy. Character Error Rate (CER) applies the same Levenshtein-distance computation at the character level instead, which makes it less sensitive to tokenisation differences and somewhat more informative for morphologically rich languages such as French and German, where a single incorrect inflection can count as a full word error under WER but only a small fractional error under CER. Both metrics were computed for this thesis using a self-contained pure-Python Levenshtein implementation rather than an external scoring package, so that the exact normalisation rules (case-folding, punctuation handling) are known and auditable rather than inherited from a third-party default.

A short worked example makes the computation concrete. Given a reference "the meeting starts at nine" (five words) and a hypothesis "the meeting start at nine a m" (seven words), word-level alignment finds one substitution ("starts" for "start"), zero deletions, and two insertions ("a", "m"), giving WER = (1 + 0 + 2) / 5 = 0.60, or 60%. CER applies the identical edit-distance computation at the character level instead: most of the letters in "starts" and "start" still match, so that one substitution contributes far less than a full character's worth of error relative to the much larger character count of the reference, while the two inserted single-character tokens still count as two character-level insertions. This is why CER and WER can diverge substantially for the same transcript pair, and is part of the motivation for reporting both throughout Chapter 6 rather than WER alone.

Real-Time Factor (RTF) is the ratio of processing time to audio duration; an RTF below 1.0 means the system transcribes faster than the audio plays, which is the practical threshold for near-real-time or batch-but-fast use. RTF depends heavily on model size, hardware, and whether GPU acceleration is available, and the measurements reported in Chapter 6 are specific to the CPU-only sandbox environment used for this thesis; they should not be read as representative of GPU-accelerated deployments. Other metrics exist in the literature, including Diarization Error Rate (DER) for speaker attribution and Semantic Error Rate variants that weight errors by their effect on meaning rather than treating all word errors equally; this thesis does not report DER or semantic-weighted metrics, because the evaluation pipeline built for it does not include a diarization-accuracy benchmark or a semantic-scoring component, a scope limitation discussed further in Chapter 5.

## 2.3 Characteristics of Corporate Meetings

Several features distinguish corporate meeting audio from the curated benchmark datasets, such as LibriSpeech or Common Voice read-speech, on which many published ASR accuracy figures are obtained. Meetings are frequently multilingual at the level of individual utterances or even within a single sentence (code-switching), particularly in organisations with a francophone and anglophone workforce. Speakers vary widely in accent, speaking rate, and microphone quality, especially in hybrid meetings where some participants join from a meeting room and others from a personal laptop in a noisy environment. Background noise sources are varied and often non-stationary: HVAC hum, keyboard typing, overlapping side conversations, and network-induced audio artefacts from videoconferencing codecs. Meetings also make heavy use of organisation-specific vocabulary, brand names, internal project names, and proper names of colleagues, that are by construction under-represented or absent from the public corpora most ASR models are pretrained on. Finally, meetings typically require speaker attribution ("who said what") for the transcript to be useful as a record, which introduces a diarization requirement layered on top of the core transcription task. Each of these factors is a documented source of ASR error in the literature (Farahani, 2017; Yoshioka et al., 2019), and together they motivate evaluating ASR systems under conditions that more closely resemble meeting audio than clean read-speech benchmarks, which is the approach taken in Chapter 5.

A short, illustrative example, constructed for explanatory purposes rather than transcribed from any real recording, conveys the code-switching difficulty concretely: a sentence such as "donc on va update le forecast, parce que la demand a été revised down for Q3" mixes French function words and discourse markers with English nouns and verbs within a single sentence, a pattern common in francophone-anglophone corporate environments. Neither a purely French nor a purely English language model handles this well, since each half of the sentence is, taken on its own, perfectly fluent in a different language. Whisper's automatic language identification, discussed further in Section 3.2 and measured directly in Section 6.4, makes one language decision per processed segment; an utterance that switches language mid-sentence is therefore a case the architecture does not natively resolve, independently of how accurately that single per-segment decision happens to be made. Architectural work on this exact problem exists: joint models that perform language identification and recognition within a single RNN-Transducer, rather than treating language identification as a separate upstream decision, have been proposed specifically to support dynamic, within-utterance language switching of the kind illustrated above (Punjabi et al., 2021); Whisper's architecture, by contrast, commits to one language per processed segment rather than modelling the switch jointly, which is the architectural root of the difficulty described in this section.

## 2.4 Accessibility in the Corporate Environment

For employees with hearing impairments, meeting transcription is not a convenience feature but frequently the primary channel through which meeting content is accessible at all. The accessibility literature on ASR-based captioning emphasises that raw transcription accuracy, while necessary, is not sufficient: latency, the readability of segment boundaries, and the visibility of transcription errors all affect whether a captioning system is actually usable in practice, and persistent ASR errors can measurably reduce how listeners or readers perceive both the speaker and the content of a meeting (Kadoma et al., 2026). This has a direct, practical implication for the present work: a system that is accurate on average but produces occasional, confident-looking, wrong transcriptions of names or technical terms can be more disruptive to a hearing-impaired user than a system with a slightly higher average error rate but more predictable failure modes, because incorrect proper nouns are difficult for a reader to catch and correct from context alone. This consideration is one of the reasons Chapter 5 evaluates domain-vocabulary prompting as a candidate mitigation, and why Chapter 6 reports not just aggregate WER but qualitative examples of the kinds of errors the evaluated system actually produces.

Caption usability research distinguishes several dimensions beyond raw word-level accuracy that determine whether a transcript is actually useful to a hearing-impaired reader in real time: the latency between speech and caption display, the visual stability of partial captions as more audio context arrives and earlier words are sometimes revised, and the presence of clear segment or speaker boundaries so a reader can follow who is speaking without relying on voice alone. Workplace accessibility for hearing-impaired employees is also shaped by physical acoustic conditions and not only by the technology used to caption them; noise-exposure and intelligibility research in occupational settings has separately examined how background noise interacts with hearing loss to affect comprehension even with assistive equipment in place (Giguère et al., 2010), which reinforces that the noise-robustness problem studied in this thesis (Chapters 5 and 6) is not a purely technical curiosity but connects to a real, previously documented accessibility concern. A growing number of jurisdictions also place legal accessibility obligations on employers regarding workplace communication; the specific instruments vary by country and sector and are outside the scope of this thesis to enumerate, but the general principle, that ASR-based captioning sits within a broader, externally motivated accessibility obligation rather than a purely discretionary feature, is part of why this thesis treats transcription-error analysis (Section 6.3 in particular) as more than a narrowly technical exercise.

# 3. Literature Review

## 3.1 The Evolution of ASR Architectures

Automatic speech recognition has moved through three broad architectural generations. The first, dominant from the 1980s through the early 2010s, combined Gaussian Mixture Models with Hidden Markov Models (GMM-HMM) to model the relationship between acoustic features and phonetic states, with a separately trained n-gram language model providing word-sequence probabilities during decoding (Li, Deng, Gong, and Haeb-Umbach, 2014). The second generation replaced the acoustic model with a deep neural network while retaining the HMM's temporal structure (DNN-HMM), yielding substantial accuracy gains without abandoning the overall pipeline. The third and current generation discards the separately trained components in favour of end-to-end neural architectures trained jointly, typically using connectionist temporal classification (CTC), attention-based encoder-decoder models, or, increasingly, transformer architectures that capture long-range dependencies across an entire utterance through self-attention rather than the fixed-size context windows of recurrent models (Vaswani et al., 2017).

Whisper, the model family evaluated in this thesis, sits in this third generation. It is an encoder-decoder transformer trained with simple multitask supervision, jointly learning transcription, translation, language identification, and voice-activity-style segmentation, on a reported 680,000 hours of weakly labelled, web-scraped audio spanning many languages (Radford et al., 2023). This scale of weak supervision is what gives Whisper its broad multilingual coverage and relative robustness to accent variation, but it also means the model's training distribution is, by the authors' own description, heavily skewed toward whatever audio happened to be abundant and transcribable on the public web, which does not obviously include corporate meeting recordings, domain-specific brand vocabulary, or the particular noise profile of a videoconferencing codec. WhisperX extends the base Whisper decoder with forced phoneme alignment (drawing on wav2vec 2.0) to produce more accurate word-level timestamps and to support downstream speaker diarisation (Bain et al., 2023); this thesis does not implement WhisperX-style forced alignment, but the gap is noted in Section 4.3 as a direction the as-built pipeline does not currently cover.

wav2vec 2.0 represents the alternative, self-supervised branch of this third generation. Rather than learning directly from audio-transcript pairs, it pretrains a contrastive objective on unlabelled audio to learn general acoustic representations, which are then fine-tuned on a comparatively small labelled dataset for a specific downstream task (Baevski et al., 2020). This approach is attractive when labelled, domain-matched audio is scarce, which is arguably the case for Nestlé-specific corporate meeting speech; however, fine-tuning wav2vec 2.0 on such data was outside the scope of the present thesis, both because no sufficiently large labelled in-domain corpus existed and because the project's CPU-only sandbox environment was not well suited to the GPU-bound fine-tuning workload this approach requires. The Conformer architecture, which augments transformer self-attention with convolutional sub-layers to better capture local acoustic patterns, is a third relevant design and has reported strong results on standard ASR benchmarks (Gulati et al., 2020), but, like wav2vec 2.0 fine-tuning, was not implemented or benchmarked directly in this thesis; it is discussed here as architectural context rather than as an evaluated system.

## 3.2 Streaming and Low-Latency ASR

A separate design axis, largely orthogonal to model architecture, is whether a system transcribes audio in a single batch pass after recording ends or incrementally as audio arrives. Whisper, in its standard form, is a batch model: it processes a complete (or chunked) audio segment and is not natively designed for frame-synchronous streaming. Architectures purpose-built for streaming, such as the RNN-Transducer and its transformer-based variants (Moritz, Hori, and Le, 2020; Tsunoo, Kashiwagi, and Watanabe, 2021; Chen et al., 2021), instead emit partial hypotheses as audio arrives, which is necessary for genuinely live captioning. Sainath et al. (2020) report an on-device streaming model that matches or exceeds server-side batch quality while running with substantially lower latency, illustrating that the streaming/batch trade-off is not purely a quality sacrifice if the architecture is designed for it from the outset. Other work in this space targets multi-speaker streaming directly, combining an RNN-Transducer with online speaker tracking so that overlapping or rapidly alternating speakers can be transcribed without waiting for a full utterance to end (Sklyar, Piunova, and Liu, 2021), and edge-deployment work has reported sub-second end-to-end latency for streaming transcription on constrained, non-server hardware (Di Leo, De Cicco, and Mascolo, 2025), which is the latency regime a genuinely live corporate-meeting captioning feature would need to target. A more recent and architecturally distinct line of work proposes unifying the audio front end directly inside a large language model to support full-duplex interaction, listening and generating speech concurrently rather than alternating between a clearly delimited input turn and output turn (Li, Wu, Hou, and Li, 2026); such systems blur the streaming-versus-batch distinction discussed above altogether, but are substantially more complex than anything evaluated in this thesis and are noted here only as an indication of where the field is moving, rather than as a system this thesis builds on or compares against. The system built for this thesis uses faster-whisper in a batch configuration for the dataset evaluation reported in Chapters 5 and 6, and separately exposes a WebSocket-based live-transcription endpoint (`/ws/transcribe`) for interactive use. That WebSocket path applies voice-activity detection to decide when an utterance has ended and then runs the same batch-style faster-whisper call on the resulting buffered segment, rather than using a purpose-built frame-synchronous streaming architecture of the kind described above; this is a reasonable engineering compromise for a pilot system; its specific mechanics, and the limitations that follow from it, are described in Section 4.6.

## 3.3 Noise-Robust ASR

Noise robustness has been approached at several points in the ASR pipeline. Classical signal-processing techniques such as spectral subtraction and Wiener filtering estimate and remove an additive noise spectrum from the input signal before recognition; more recent neural noise-suppression methods, including the `noisereduce` library used in this thesis, apply a similar spectral-gating idea but estimate the noise profile adaptively from the signal itself. Feature-domain robustness techniques instead try to make the acoustic features themselves less sensitive to noise, for instance by modelling the expected distortion directly in the short-time Fourier transform domain (Astudillo and Orglmeister, 2013), or by adapting acoustic models to a noisy-speech distribution rather than only a clean one (Li, Deng, Gong, and Haeb-Umbach, 2014). Other approaches operate earlier still, using autocorrelation-domain feature extraction designed to be inherently less sensitive to additive noise (Farahani, 2017). A further, complementary technique augments the training data itself rather than the model or the input features, applying randomised time and frequency masking directly to spectrograms during training so the acoustic model learns representations that are inherently more robust to partial information loss, a technique shown to improve recognition accuracy even without any explicit noise model (Park et al., 2019). Other adaptation-based approaches retrain or adjust the acoustic model itself on noisy or noise-augmented speech so that the model's internal representation of "what speech sounds like" already accounts for the target noise distribution, rather than trying to clean the signal beforehand (Li, Deng, Gong, and Haeb-Umbach, 2014); related work on prosody modification and acoustic adaptation reports similar gains from adjusting models toward the prosodic characteristics of the target deployment condition rather than the condition the model happened to be trained on (Bhardwaj, Kukreja, and Singh, 2021). Closely related work on low-resource African languages has proposed end-to-end noise-robust frameworks specifically because standard ASR pipelines, tuned for high-resource languages and clean training data, transfer particularly poorly when both noise and limited training data are present at once (Ejigu, Asfaw, and Tadesse, 2025); the methodological parallel to this thesis is direct, since the present pilot also combines added noise with comparatively small, non-Nestlé-domain training and evaluation data. A final, hardware-level approach, beamforming with multi-microphone arrays, spatially filters sound to attenuate noise sources outside the direction of the active speaker, an approach that has been combined with location-guided neural extraction networks to recognise overlapped multi-speaker speech directly from multi-channel input (Chen et al., 2018); this requires multi-channel audio capture and was not applicable to the single-channel recordings used in this thesis's evaluation, but would be a natural complement to the software-level techniques discussed here in a meeting-room deployment with a known microphone array.

Voice activity detection (VAD) is a complementary, lighter-weight technique: rather than improving recognition of noisy speech directly, it discards segments classified as non-speech before they ever reach the ASR model, which reduces both wasted computation and the chance of the model hallucinating text from silence or noise. Silero VAD is a commonly used open-source neural VAD model for this purpose and is the model named in this project's codebase; whether and where it is actually exercised by the implemented system is addressed directly in Section 4.6 and again in Chapter 5, rather than assumed.

## 3.4 Domain Adaptation and Vocabulary Customisation

Generic ASR models systematically under-perform on vocabulary that is rare or absent in their training data, which includes most organisation-specific brand names, product codes, and the names of individual colleagues. Two broad mitigation strategies exist. The first is fine-tuning: continuing to train a pretrained model on labelled, domain-matched audio so that its weights directly encode the target vocabulary and acoustic conditions; this has been shown to be effective for adapting Whisper to specific languages and accents under-represented in its original training mix, including agglutinative and dialectal varieties whose phonology differs substantially from the high-resource languages that dominate Whisper's training data (Daouad, Ataa Allah, and Dadi, 2024; Polat et al., 2024); a broader systematic comparison of fine-tuning strategies across several low-resource languages with only limited supervised data reaches a consistent conclusion, that targeted fine-tuning narrows but does not always eliminate the gap to Whisper's high-resource performance, with the achievable improvement depending heavily on how much labelled target-domain audio is actually available (Liu, Yang, and Qu, 2024). Full fine-tuning of a model with hundreds of millions or billions of parameters is itself computationally demanding, which has motivated parameter-efficient alternatives such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), where only a small number of additional low-rank weight matrices are trained and added to an otherwise frozen pretrained model; this has been shown to recover much of the accuracy benefit of full fine-tuning, including for child-speech recognition, a domain with its own acoustic mismatch from typical adult-speech training data, at a fraction of the training cost (Ankita et al., 2026). The second strategy, used in this thesis, is decode-time prompting: supplying a short text prompt before decoding begins so the model's existing language-modelling capacity is nudged toward expected vocabulary, without any additional training. This is considerably cheaper than fine-tuning, since it requires no labelled audio and no GPU training run, but it is also a weaker intervention, because it does not change the model's underlying acoustic representations and therefore cannot help with terms the model would struggle to recognise acoustically regardless of context. A further line of work questions a basic assumption behind benchmark-driven domain-adaptation claims: synthetic or narrowly scoped fine-tuning data can overstate real-world gains if the conditions under which a model is tested do not match the conditions it will actually be deployed in, which is itself a caution directly relevant to this thesis's own use of a substitute, non-Nestlé-domain dataset, discussed in Chapter 5 (Tran et al., 2025). Custom-vocabulary benchmarks built specifically to probe rare or unseen named entities exist precisely because aggregate WER on a generic test set can hide poor performance on exactly the named entities an organisation cares most about, brand names, executive names, internal project codes, since such entities are typically a small fraction of total reference words and therefore have only a small influence on an aggregate score even when they are recognised badly almost every time they occur (Durmus et al., 2026). This last point is the central methodological reason this thesis reports qualitative, example-level transcription errors alongside aggregate WER in Chapter 6, rather than treating a single aggregate number as sufficient evidence about named-entity recognition specifically. The decode-time prompting approach evaluated in this thesis is, in this taxonomy, the cheapest and weakest of the available domain-adaptation strategies: unlike fine-tuning, it cannot change how the acoustic model represents a target word it has never reliably heard before, it can only make an already-plausible candidate word more likely to be selected during decoding, which is consistent with the small, dataset-constrained net effect this thesis actually measures for it in Chapter 6.

## 3.5 Speaker Diarization and Multi-Speaker Meetings

Meeting transcription introduces a second task beyond word recognition: attributing each segment of speech to the correct speaker. Clustering-based diarization pipelines typically extract a fixed-length speaker-embedding vector from each short window of audio and then apply an unsupervised clustering algorithm, commonly agglomerative hierarchical clustering, to group windows believed to come from the same speaker, with the number of clusters either fixed in advance or estimated from the data. More recent end-to-end neural diarization models, such as the pyannote.audio toolkit used in this project's codebase, instead learn speaker segmentation and clustering jointly within a single neural pipeline, which has been reported to improve robustness specifically on short speaker turns and on transitions where clustering-based methods are prone to oversegmentation (Bredin et al., 2020); related local-global network designs pursue the same end-to-end goal specifically for a variable, not-known-in-advance number of speakers, using discriminative speaker embeddings learned jointly with segmentation rather than clustered afterward (Maiti et al., 2021). The standard evaluation metric for this task, Diarization Error Rate (DER), aggregates missed speech, false-alarm speech, and speaker-confusion error into a single percentage; this thesis does not report DER, both because the diarization component was not exercised during the pilot evaluation (Section 4.3) and because, as already noted in Section 2.2, doing so meaningfully would require multi-speaker reference annotations that the substitute dataset used in this thesis does not provide. Diarization performance degrades substantially under overlapping speech, which is common in unstructured meeting conversation and is one of the most actively studied open problems in the field, since most embedding-extraction windows implicitly assume a single dominant speaker per window. Integrating diarization with ASR output, rather than treating the two as independent post-processed layers, as the implementation described in Section 4.2 does, has been shown to improve attribution accuracy in dedicated meeting-transcription systems that instead model separation, diarisation, and recognition jointly or in tighter feedback (Raj et al., 2021; Yoshioka et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2021). The system built for this thesis follows the simpler, post-processing pattern: faster-whisper produces timestamped segments independently, and a pyannote diarization pass, when available, is aligned to those segments by maximal temporal overlap rather than through joint modelling. This is a deliberate scope reduction appropriate to a pilot study, and it is also gated behind the availability of a Hugging Face access token for the pyannote model weights; no diarization-accuracy evaluation is reported in Chapter 6, both because of this scope reduction and because the pilot evaluation dataset described in Chapter 5 consists of short, largely single-speaker utterances rather than multi-speaker meeting recordings.

## 3.6 Accessibility, Synthesis, and the Research Gap

The accessibility literature provides the normative grounding for why ASR error matters beyond a raw accuracy number. Persistent transcription errors have been shown to measurably affect how listeners or readers judge both the content and the speaker of a recording (Kadoma, Shrivastava, and Naaman, 2026). More broadly, assistive-technology and accessibility research consistently treats raw transcription accuracy as only one of several variables determining whether a captioning or speech-to-text tool is actually experienced as helpful, alongside factors such as latency, error severity, and how easily a reader can recover the intended meaning from a partially garbled transcript; comparable observations recur in both workplace-assistive-technology contexts and in education-focused work on hearing-impaired learners, where the pedagogical and psychological dimensions of spoken-language access, confidence, participation, and inclusion, are treated as inseparable from technical accuracy alone. That broader literature most often concerns classroom or everyday-life settings rather than a corporate meeting specifically; this thesis does not survey it exhaustively and treats the point as a normative motivation for measuring WER and CER rather than as an empirically established finding specific to corporate settings, but the underlying claim, that error rate alone does not determine whether an assistive technology is experienced as helpful, applies directly to the corporate-meeting use case considered here as well.

Taken together, the literature reviewed in this chapter suggests a research gap that is narrow but practically important: there is substantial published work on noise-robust ASR, on domain adaptation, and on diarisation individually, each typically evaluated on its own benchmark and often on read or broadcast speech rather than spontaneous corporate conversation, but comparatively little public, reproducible evidence on how a single, realistically deployable, CPU-only pipeline behaves when noise, multilingual code-switching, and domain vocabulary are all present at once, in the kind of meeting audio a hearing-impaired employee would actually need transcribed. Most of the individual techniques surveyed above, noise reduction, domain-vocabulary prompting, forced language identification, larger model checkpoints, are each well studied on their own; what is comparatively rare is a single evaluation that holds the rest of the pipeline fixed and measures each technique's marginal effect on the same dataset, which is precisely the design adopted in Chapter 5. This thesis does not claim to close the broader gap definitively, and is explicit about not doing so: it reports a small, honestly scoped pilot evaluation aimed at that intersection, with the dataset-size and scope caveats detailed in Chapter 5 treated as first-class limitations rather than minimised.

# 4. System Architecture

## 4.1 Platform Overview

The system implemented for this thesis is one component of a larger three-topic platform built collaboratively for the Nestlé/BIT accessibility project: an OCR pipeline for scanned corporate documents (Topic 1), the speech-to-text pipeline that is the subject of this thesis (Topic 2), and a retrieval-augmented question-answering layer over both (Topic 3). All three are exposed through a single FastAPI backend (`backend/main.py`), which loads the Whisper model and initialises a ChromaDB vector store on startup, and which currently runs entirely on local, open-source components: faster-whisper for transcription, a local Tesseract-based OCR stub for documents, `sentence-transformers` (the multilingual `intfloat/multilingual-e5-small` model) for embeddings, ChromaDB as the vector store, and a locally hosted Ollama model for answer generation. The codebase explicitly labels this as a "Phase 1" configuration, with inline comments marking specific Azure-managed services (Azure AI Speech, Azure Document Intelligence, Azure AI Search, Azure OpenAI) as a planned "Phase 2 migration" for each component. This thesis evaluates the Phase 1, locally hosted STT pipeline; the Phase 2 Azure migration is discussed in Chapter 7 as a deployment proposal, not as a system that was built, deployed, or measured.

The FastAPI application itself (`app = FastAPI(...)`) is configured with permissive cross-origin resource sharing (`CORSMiddleware`, `allow_origins=["*"]`, `allow_methods=["*"]`, `allow_headers=["*"]`), and its startup is governed by an asynchronous `lifespan` context manager that pre-loads, in sequence, the cached faster-whisper model, the ChromaDB collection and its embedding model, the Silero VAD model, and the pyannote diarisation pipeline, logging a warning rather than failing outright if any individual component cannot be pre-loaded (for instance, when no Hugging Face token is available for pyannote). This pre-loading pattern means the first request after startup does not pay the one-off model-loading cost that an on-demand `load_model()` call would otherwise impose, which matters in particular for the live WebSocket path described in Section 4.6, where a per-connection load would make the first few seconds of every session unresponsive. The permissive CORS configuration is appropriate for a single-tenant pilot deployment exercised from a known frontend during development, but is revisited as a security consideration in Section 7.3, since it does not, on its own, distinguish a legitimate frontend origin from any other origin that might call the API directly.

## 4.2 The STT Pipeline

The core transcription path lives in `backend/stt/engine.py` and is organised around a `TranscribeConfig` dataclass that centralises the options exposed elsewhere as keyword arguments: model size, target language (or `None` for automatic detection), task (transcription or translation), whether noise-reduction preprocessing is applied, whether a domain-vocabulary prompt is injected, and decoding options such as word-level timestamps. A call to `transcribe_file()` optionally runs `preprocess_audio()`, which resamples the input to 16kHz mono and applies spectral-gating noise reduction via the `noisereduce` library, before passing the (possibly cleaned) audio to a cached `faster_whisper.WhisperModel` instance. The model runs with INT8 quantisation when no CUDA device is available, which is the case throughout this thesis's evaluation environment, and with float16 precision on GPU when one is present. Decoding options are assembled conditionally: a `language` key is only added when a specific language is forced, and an `initial_prompt` key, set to a fixed string of Nestlé-related brand and vocabulary terms (`DEFAULT_DOMAIN_PROMPT`), is only added when domain-prompt injection is enabled. The function returns the concatenated transcript text, a list of timestamped segments, the language Whisper's internal detector reported (`info.language`), the wall-clock processing duration, and the model name actually used.

Concretely, `TranscribeConfig` defaults to a `model_size` read from the `WHISPER_MODEL` environment variable (falling back to `small` if unset), `language=None` (automatic detection), `task="transcribe"`, `preprocessing=True`, `domain_prompt=True` with `domain_prompt_text` set to the module-level `DEFAULT_DOMAIN_PROMPT` constant, `word_timestamps=True`, and `condition_on_previous_text=True`. The pilot evaluation harness described in Chapter 5 overrides these defaults explicitly per condition rather than relying on them. The live WebSocket transcription path described in Section 4.6, by contrast, does not construct a `TranscribeConfig` at all and calls the cached model's `transcribe()` method directly with its own, separately hardcoded options, a structural distinction that matters directly for the gap discussed in that section.

## 4.3 Preprocessing, Diarization, and Two Implementation Gaps

Two components of the architecture as originally designed are present in the codebase but not fully wired into the path actually exercised by `transcribe_file()`, and both are reported here rather than glossed over, since they directly shaped what the evaluation in Chapter 5 could and could not measure. First, `load_vad_model()` loads a Silero VAD model and is defined as a standalone function, but `transcribe_file()` never calls it: voice-activity detection is not actually applied before transcription in the current implementation, despite being part of the originally planned pipeline (and despite a VAD-based condition being part of the originally planned experimental matrix, discussed further in Chapter 5). Second, while Whisper's internal language identification result (`info.language`) is logged to the application log and returned in the function's output dictionary, the evaluation harness used for the pilot study (described in Chapter 5) does not persist this value into its results table; the detected language per run had to be recovered indirectly, by comparing hypothesis text between automatic-detection and forced-language runs of the same file, rather than read directly from a logged field. Speaker diarisation, by contrast, is implemented as designed: when a Hugging Face access token is present and preprocessing is enabled, `transcribe_file()` loads a pyannote speaker-diarisation pipeline and aligns each Whisper segment to the diarisation turn with which it has the greatest temporal overlap. Diarisation was not exercised during the pilot evaluation in Chapter 5, both because no Hugging Face token was configured in the evaluation environment and because the evaluation dataset is composed of short, single-speaker utterances for which diarisation accuracy is not a meaningful measurement.

## 4.4 API Surface

The backend exposes a small number of HTTP and WebSocket endpoints relevant to this thesis. `POST /transcribe` accepts an uploaded audio or video file, runs it through the STT pipeline described above, and indexes the resulting transcript into the shared ChromaDB collection for later retrieval. `WS /ws/transcribe` is the live-transcription counterpart, accepting streamed audio over a WebSocket connection for interactive use. `POST /ocr` and `POST /ask` belong to Topics 1 and 3 respectively but share the same backend process and vector store; `/ask` answers a natural-language question by retrieving relevant chunks from across both OCR and STT-derived content and passing them, together with the question, to the locally hosted language model. `GET /health` reports basic service status for monitoring. These are the endpoints that actually exist in the codebase at the time of writing; an earlier internal description of this API circulated during the project (reproduced, with corrections, in Appendix B) additionally referenced `/summarize` and `/custom-vocabulary` endpoints that do not exist in the implementation and have been removed from the corrected version.

`POST /transcribe` accepts an optional `language` query parameter that, when supplied, is passed through to `transcribe_file()` as `forced_language` and forces Whisper's language rather than relying on automatic detection, which is the mechanism Condition E in Chapter 5 exercises programmatically rather than through the HTTP layer. Internally, the endpoint generates a random `uuid4` document identifier, validates the upload's file extension against a fixed allow-list of audio and video container formats, writes the file to a local `storage/uploads/` directory before transcription, and, after transcription completes, ingests the resulting text into the shared ChromaDB collection via `rag.vectorstore.ingest()`, returning the document identifier, filename, detected or forced language, full transcript text, per-segment list, audio duration, the Whisper model size actually used, and a boolean and count indicating how many chunks were ingested for retrieval. `GET /health` returns a small JSON object reporting a fixed `"status": "ok"`, whether a local Ollama instance answered an `/api/tags` request within a three-second timeout, the Whisper model size configured via the `WHISPER_MODEL` environment variable, the configured `LLM_PROVIDER`, and the current ChromaDB collection row count; it is a liveness and basic-dependency check rather than a deep health probe, and it does not verify that the STT or diarisation models specifically have finished loading; a `/health` call made before the `lifespan` pre-loading step has completed faster-whisper or pyannote can still return `"status": "ok"`. Two additional routes, `GET /` and `GET /live`, serve the project's two HTML frontend pages (a file-upload transcription page and a live-recording page) directly via `FileResponse` and are marked `include_in_schema=False` so they do not appear in the generated OpenAPI documentation; despite an unused `StaticFiles` import in the same module, the backend does not mount a conventional static-files directory, and any additional frontend assets beyond these two pages would need their own dedicated route.

## 4.5 Data Models and Response Schemas

The project defines its intended request and response shapes as Pydantic models in `backend/models.py`, grouped by the three platform topics plus a shared health schema. `TranscriptSegment` (start, end, text, optional speaker, optional confidence) mirrors the per-segment dictionaries produced by `transcribe_file()` and is the building block of `TranscribeResponse`, which additionally carries a generated document identifier, the original filename, the language used, the full transcript text, audio duration, the Whisper model size, and a boolean flag indicating whether the transcript was successfully ingested into the retrieval layer. `OcrPage` and `OcrResponse` mirror this structure for Topic 1, replacing per-segment timing with a per-page number. `AskRequest` carries a free-text question and an optional language hint (`"fr"`, `"en"`, `"de"`, or `"auto"`); `SourceChunk` records which of the two content types, `"stt"` or `"ocr"`, a retrieved chunk came from, alongside its source filename and a short excerpt; and `AskResponse` bundles the answer text with a list of these source chunks and a detected-language field. `HealthResponse` declares `status`, `ollama_available`, `whisper_model`, and `chroma_collection_count`.

These models function, in the current implementation, as documentation of the intended response shape rather than as an enforced runtime contract: none of the routes in `backend/main.py`, including `/transcribe` and `/health`, declare a FastAPI `response_model` parameter against the corresponding class, so the framework does not validate or filter outgoing JSON against these schemas, and a route handler could in principle return a dictionary with extra or missing fields without FastAPI raising an error. This is visible directly in the `/health` handler, whose actual return dictionary includes an `llm_provider` key that has no corresponding field in `HealthResponse` at all. This is a minor, low-risk gap for a single-developer pilot system where the handler code and the schema were written by the same person with the same intent in mind, but it does mean the schemas in `models.py` should be read as a design specification rather than as a guarantee about what any given endpoint will actually return at runtime, which would matter more if this codebase were extended by additional contributors who relied on `models.py` as ground truth without cross-checking the route implementations.

## 4.6 The Live WebSocket Transcription Path

`WS /ws/transcribe` is handled by a separate `RealTimeSTTHandler` class that does not reuse `transcribe_file()` or `TranscribeConfig` at all; it is a parallel, independently written code path that calls the cached Whisper model's `transcribe()` method directly with its own options. On connection, the handler checks that the global cached model and the global cached Silero VAD model are both already loaded, refusing the connection with WebSocket close code 1001 otherwise, which is consistent with the `lifespan` pre-loading strategy described in Section 4.1: a connection arriving before pre-loading has finished, or after a pre-loading failure for either model, is rejected rather than triggering an on-demand load mid-session.

Once connected, the handler receives raw 16kHz mono PCM audio as a stream of binary WebSocket frames. Each incoming chunk is converted to a normalised float32 array and passed to the Silero VAD model, which returns a single speech-probability score for that chunk. A probability above 0.5 is treated as speech: the chunk is appended both to a per-utterance buffer and to a separate, continuously growing `global_audio` buffer that persists for the lifetime of the connection, and a silence-frame counter is reset to zero. A probability at or below 0.5 while the handler considers itself mid-utterance instead increments that silence-frame counter by the chunk's sample count; once accumulated silence reaches 0.6 seconds (`0.6 * 16000` samples), the handler treats the utterance as complete, triggers transcription and diarisation on the buffered audio, and clears the per-utterance buffer for the next utterance. This is the same Silero VAD model whose absence from the batch `transcribe_file()` path is reported as an implementation gap in Section 4.3; the live path is therefore the one part of the system where VAD is genuinely exercised, and the two paths consequently differ in a way the pilot evaluation in Chapters 5 and 6, which only exercises the batch path, does not capture.

Two further implementation details are specific to this path and are reported here because they were found only by reading `RealTimeSTTHandler._transcribe_and_diarize()` directly rather than assumed from the architecture description that motivated this thesis. First, the transcription call hardcodes `language="fr"` on every invocation, regardless of the language actually being spoken, and regardless of the `language` query parameter the file-based `/transcribe` endpoint exposes for exactly this purpose; a live session conducted in English or German would still be decoded with French forced as the target language. Second, the call also hardcodes its own `initial_prompt` string inline, "Nestlé, Nespresso, Maggi, Purina, KitKat, Nescafé, supply chain, plant-based portfolio", rather than importing the shared `DEFAULT_DOMAIN_PROMPT` constant used by `transcribe_file()`; the two strings are similar but not identical, since `DEFAULT_DOMAIN_PROMPT` additionally includes "Nestlé Ghana", which means the two code paths can drift independently if one is edited without the other. Neither hardcoded value is exposed as a connection parameter, so a deployment serving meetings in more than one language would need code changes, not configuration changes, to support its English- or German-speaking users over this endpoint. Given the Section 6.4 finding that forced language correctly recovers French-script output but does not, on this dataset, improve aggregate French WER, and that automatic detection failed in roughly four in ten of the French clips tested, hardcoding French specifically is a defensible default for a French-medium pilot audience but not a multilingual one; the gap is in the absence of a parameter, not in the choice of French as a fallback.

The live path also differs from the batch path in two further respects. It never calls `preprocess_audio()` or the `noisereduce` library, so no noise-reduction is applied to streamed audio at all, unlike the optional preprocessing step in `transcribe_file()`. And its diarisation step, when a pyannote pipeline is loaded, runs not on the current utterance buffer but on the trailing ten seconds of the cumulative `global_audio` buffer (`10 * sample_rate * 2` bytes), assigning the utterance to whichever speaker label pyannote's `itertracks()` iterator returns last for that window, a simpler last-speaker-wins heuristic rather than the temporal-overlap alignment `transcribe_file()` uses for the batch path described in Section 4.3. This is a reasonable engineering shortcut for a low-latency interactive feature, where computing diarisation over the entire session history on every utterance would be increasingly expensive as a meeting lengthens, but it means live and batch diarisation are not directly comparable, and neither was evaluated for accuracy in this thesis.

## 4.7 Hardware and Runtime Environment

All development, implementation, and evaluation work for this thesis was carried out in a CPU-only sandboxed Linux environment with no GPU acceleration available, which is also the configuration assumed throughout faster-whisper's INT8 code path described in Section 4.2. No dedicated server-grade hardware (the kind of multi-core Xeon or cloud GPU instance that might be quoted in an enterprise deployment plan) was provisioned or used for this thesis; the real-time-factor figures reported in Chapter 6 should therefore be read as characterising a deliberately modest, CPU-only configuration, and as a conservative lower bound on the speed that would be available on dedicated transcription hardware, rather than as a claim about production-scale throughput.

# 5. Methodology and Experimental Design

## 5.1 Dataset Construction

Evaluating the pipeline described in Chapter 4 requires audio with known ground-truth transcripts, spanning multiple languages and at least two noise conditions. No Nestlé-internal meeting recording could be used for this purpose: the project's own dataset documentation (`datasets/stt_eval/README.md`) explicitly prohibits committing confidential or copyrighted audio to the evaluation set, which rules out the corporate meeting recordings that would be the most directly representative source. The documentation instead recommends public corpora such as Mozilla Common Voice or LibriSpeech. In practice, Common Voice, LibriSpeech, FLEURS, and VoxPopuli were all attempted first and found impractical within this project's sandboxed environment: streaming downloads repeatedly hung, and the available shards were multi-hundred-megabyte Parquet files without convenient row-level indexing for selecting a small, balanced, multilingual subset. This is reported here as a methodological constraint rather than omitted, since it explains why the dataset finally used is not one of the originally recommended corpora.

The dataset actually used is PolyAI/MInDS-14, a CC-BY-4.0-licensed corpus of real, spontaneous telephone-banking speech across fourteen languages, of which English, French, and German subsets were sampled for this thesis (Gerz et al., 2021). Twenty-four clean clips were drawn directly from the corpus, eight per language, together with their genuine reference transcripts. A further twelve noisy variants, four per language, were generated by adding synthetic white noise at a controlled 8 dB signal-to-noise ratio, the same threshold named in the project's own outline document as the boundary for a "noisy" condition, to a subset of the clean recordings, reusing the same ground-truth transcript as the underlying clean clip. This yields 36 evaluation samples in total: 24 clean and 12 noisy, evenly split across English, French, and German. Two limitations of this dataset follow directly from its substitution for the originally envisaged corpus and are treated as first-class limitations rather than footnotes. First, the speech is single-speaker, 8kHz telephone-banking audio rather than multi-speaker, full-bandwidth corporate meeting audio, so it does not exercise diarisation or the broader acoustic variability of a real meeting room. Second, it contains none of the Nestlé-specific brand or executive vocabulary the domain-prompting condition is designed to help with, which directly limits what that condition's results, reported in Chapter 6, can demonstrate. Both limitations are revisited in Chapter 8.

**Table 5.1 — Evaluation samples by language and noise condition**

| Language | Clean | Noisy | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| English (en) | 8 | 4 | 12 |
| French (fr) | 8 | 4 | 12 |
| German (de) | 8 | 4 | 12 |
| Total | 24 | 12 | 36 |

The four noisy clips per language are synthetic-noise variants of four of that language's eight clean clips rather than independently sourced recordings, so each noisy file shares its ground-truth reference transcript with a specific clean counterpart (recorded in `metadata.csv` via a shared `reference_filename` value); this was a deliberate design choice that holds linguistic content fixed while varying only the noise condition, isolating the effect of noise from the effect of which words happen to be spoken, but it also means the 36 samples represent 32 distinct underlying utterances rather than 36 independent ones, a further small-sample caveat alongside those already noted above.

## 5.2 Evaluation Framework

Word error rate, character error rate, and real-time factor (defined in Section 2.2) were computed for every transcription run using a self-contained, pure-Python Levenshtein-distance implementation (`backend/evaluation/metrics.py`) rather than an external scoring library, so that normalisation choices, lower-casing and punctuation stripping before alignment, are fixed and auditable rather than inherited as an opaque default. Each run produces one row recording the input file, language, noise condition, model size, whether preprocessing and domain prompting were enabled, the forced language (if any), audio duration, processing time, WER, CER, and RTF, together with the full reference and hypothesis text, which makes it possible to inspect individual transcription errors rather than relying on aggregate numbers alone; this per-file inspection is what produced the qualitative examples reported in Chapter 6.

Concretely, `normalize_text()` in `backend/evaluation/metrics.py` lower-cases both reference and hypothesis, strips a fixed punctuation set (Python's `string.punctuation` plus the curly quotation marks and em/en dashes that the project's reference transcripts occasionally contain), and collapses repeated whitespace to a single space, before either string is split into words for WER or into characters, with spaces removed, for CER. The underlying edit-distance routine is a standard Wagner-Fischer dynamic-programming implementation that also backtracks through the completed cost matrix to classify each edit operation as a substitution, deletion, or insertion individually, rather than returning only a single aggregate edit-distance number; this is what allows WER to be reported as the precise (S + D + I) / N defined in Section 2.2 rather than an approximation. Both `wer()` and `cer()` degrade to a defined value rather than raising an exception on an empty reference string, returning 0.0 when the hypothesis is also empty and 1.0 otherwise, which avoids a divide-by-zero failure on any pathological zero-length transcript without silently dropping that run from the aggregate statistics in Table 6.1.

Samples themselves are loaded through a small, explicitly validated pipeline rather than read ad hoc at evaluation time. `load_metadata()` (`backend/evaluation/dataset.py`) parses `metadata.csv` into a list of `EvalSample` dataclass instances, checking first that the five required columns (`audio_filename`, `reference_filename`, `language`, `noise_condition`, `speaker_count`) are all present and raising a `ValueError` naming the specific missing columns otherwise, rather than failing later with a confusing `KeyError` deep inside the evaluation loop. A separate `validate_samples()` function then checks, for every loaded sample, that both the resolved audio path and the resolved reference path actually exist on disk, returning a list of human-readable warning strings rather than raising immediately; `run_pilot_matrix.py` logs each such warning and then explicitly filters the sample list down to only those samples whose audio and reference files were both confirmed to exist before entering the main evaluation loop. This two-step load-then-validate pattern means a missing or misnamed file produces an explicit, logged warning rather than either crashing the entire 180-run matrix partway through or, worse, silently scoring a sample against an empty or wrong reference transcript.

## 5.3 Experimental Conditions

Five experimental conditions were run, each applied to all 36 dataset samples, for a total of 180 transcription runs:

Condition A (baseline) uses the `tiny` faster-whisper model with no preprocessing and no domain prompt. Condition B adds noise-reduction preprocessing (`noisereduce`) on top of the baseline. Condition D adds domain-vocabulary prompt injection on top of B. Condition E forces the language parameter to each sample's true language, on top of D, instead of relying on automatic language detection. Condition F holds preprocessing on and prompting off, as in B, but substitutes the larger `base` model for `tiny`, isolating the effect of model size.

**Table 5.2 — Experimental conditions (n=36 samples per condition, 180 runs total)**

| Tag | Model | Preprocessing | Domain prompt | Forced language |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A — Baseline | tiny | off | off | none (auto-detect) |
| B — Noise reduction | tiny | on | off | none (auto-detect) |
| D — Domain prompt | tiny | on | on | none (auto-detect) |
| E — Forced language | tiny | on | on | sample's true language |
| F — Base model | base | on | off | none (auto-detect) |

Each condition is a single, named dictionary entry in `backend/evaluation/run_pilot_matrix.py`, deliberately separate from the more generic, auto-generated `condition_tag` string (`prep{0|1}_prompt{0|1}_lang{auto|xx}`) that the lower-level `run_single()` function in `backend/evaluation/runner.py` builds for its own per-file transcript filenames; the pilot script's named tags (A, B, D, E, F) are what Chapter 6 and Table 6.1 actually report against. The matrix script is deliberately resumable: it appends each completed row to `results/stt_eval_pilot_results.csv` immediately rather than holding results in memory until the end, and on every invocation it first loads the set of `(condition_tag, filename)` pairs already present in that file and skips them, which let the 180-run matrix be completed across multiple short, separately time-boxed invocations (each capped via a `--max-seconds` argument) without re-running, or losing the results of, work already done.

Conditions A through E form a deliberate, cumulative chain rather than five independent configurations: B adds noise-reduction preprocessing on top of A, D adds domain-vocabulary prompting on top of B, and E adds forced language identification on top of D, so that comparing any adjacent pair in this chain (A vs. B, B vs. D, D vs. E) isolates the marginal effect of exactly one additional intervention while holding everything upstream of it fixed. Condition F is the one exception to this chain: it holds preprocessing on and prompting off, matching Condition B's configuration exactly except for substituting the `base` model for `tiny`, which makes F vs. B the cleanest available comparison for isolating model size specifically, rather than F vs. any other condition in the chain. This chained design is what allows Chapter 6 to attribute each observed change in WER, CER, or RTF to a single, named methodological choice, noise reduction, domain prompting, language forcing, or model size, rather than to an unspecified combination of several simultaneous changes, and it is the reason the conditions are presented and discussed in that specific order, A through F, in both this chapter and the next.

A sixth condition originally planned in the project's outline, voice-activity-detection preprocessing using Silero VAD (Condition C), was not run. As documented in Section 4.3, the VAD component is defined in the codebase but never invoked by the transcription path actually exercised by the pipeline; rather than implement a one-off VAD call solely to produce a number for this chapter, or silently drop the condition without comment, its absence is reported here as a methodological finding: it indicates that VAD-based preprocessing, despite being part of the original design, was not in fact part of the system being evaluated. All 180 runs completed without a runtime error or a missing or invalid metric value, which is itself worth recording given how much can silently go wrong in an audio-processing pipeline (empty transcripts, divide-by-zero on zero-length references, multiprocessing failures); one genuine implementation bug was found and fixed during this evaluation, an `import torch` statement incorrectly nested inside a `try/except ImportError` block for an unrelated optional dependency, which would otherwise have raised a `NameError` whenever that dependency was absent.

## 5.4 Evaluation Hypotheses

Four hypotheses were formulated before running the evaluation, directly corresponding to the research questions in Section 1.4, and are stated here as originally framed so that Chapter 6 can report against them rather than reverse-engineering hypotheses from favourable results. H1: noise-reduction preprocessing reduces WER on noisy audio without materially increasing WER on clean audio. H2: domain-vocabulary prompting reduces WER specifically on named entities, with a smaller or negligible effect on general transcription accuracy. H3: the CPU-only pipeline achieves a real-time factor below 1.0 across the tested model sizes. H4: forcing the correct language outperforms automatic language detection, particularly on short or noisy clips. Chapter 6 reports, for each hypothesis, whether the pilot data support it, partially support it, or do not allow it to be tested as originally framed, which is the case for H2 given the dataset limitation described in Section 5.1.

This pre-registration discipline is not merely a methodological gesture: the four hypotheses above, and the five conditions actually run, are drawn essentially verbatim from `Topic2_Outline_Reference.md`, a project planning document that predates the pilot evaluation run reported in Chapter 6. Comparing the two documents directly shows both continuity and one deliberate, disclosed departure. Conditions A, B, D, and E in the outline correspond directly to Conditions A, B, D, and E actually run; Condition C (Silero VAD preprocessing) was dropped for the methodological reason already given in Section 5.3, not silently, and Condition F was originally specified in the outline as a `small`/`medium`/`large-v2` model-size comparison but was run in this thesis as `tiny`/`base` instead, again for the disclosed sandbox-hardware reason given in Section 2.1, preserving the comparison's intent, model size versus accuracy and speed, while substituting feasible checkpoint sizes for the originally envisaged ones. Because the hypotheses were fixed in writing before any transcription run was performed, Chapter 6's hypothesis-by-hypothesis verdicts in Section 6.6, including the two, H1 and H2, that turned out to be only partially supported or effectively untestable as originally framed, are evaluated against a fixed target rather than against expectations adjusted after seeing the data.

## 5.5 Hardware and Runtime Environment

All 180 runs were executed in a CPU-only sandboxed environment (Ubuntu 22.04, Python 3.10) with no GPU available, using faster-whisper's INT8 quantised CPU code path described in Section 4.2. No dedicated benchmarking hardware, multi-core server CPUs, GPU instances, or controlled thermal/load conditions, was used; the sandbox was a shared, general-purpose compute environment, and other background processes may have intermittently competed for CPU time during the run. Each (condition, file) pair was run exactly once rather than averaged over repeated trials, which is acceptable for WER and CER, since these are deterministic given fixed model weights and decoding settings, but means the RTF values reported in Chapter 6 should be read as indicative of typical throughput on this hardware rather than as precise, noise-free laboratory measurements.

## 5.6 Reproducibility

Several concrete features of the evaluation harness support independent reproduction or re-scoring of the pilot results reported in Chapter 6, beyond the dataset and metric definitions already given above. Every individual transcription run writes its hypothesis text to its own file in `results/transcripts/`, named from the source filename and the condition tag, in addition to being recorded as a row in the aggregate results CSV; that CSV row itself carries the full reference and hypothesis text alongside the computed WER, CER, and RTF, so any reported number in Chapter 6 can be traced back to the exact text pair that produced it and, if desired, independently re-scored with a different metrics implementation. Decoding itself is deterministic given fixed model weights and fixed input audio, since this thesis's evaluation runs do not enable Whisper's temperature-sampling fallback path, so re-running a given (condition, file) pair on the same hardware and library versions is expected to reproduce the same hypothesis text rather than a randomly varying one. Exact dependency versions are pinned as lower bounds in `pyproject.toml`: `faster-whisper>=1.2.1`, `openai-whisper>=20231117`, `noisereduce>=3.0.2`, `pyannote-audio>=4.0.4`, `librosa>=0.10.1`, and `soundfile>=0.12.1`, under Python `>=3.10,<3.14`; the project additionally pins exact PyTorch build versions (`torch==2.6.0+cu124`, `torchvision==0.21.0+cu124`, `torchaudio==2.6.0+cu124`) sourced from PyTorch's own CUDA 12.4 wheel index, which is notable because the pilot evaluation reported in Chapter 6 was nonetheless run entirely on CPU-only hardware, since faster-whisper's INT8 CPU code path does not require the CUDA build of PyTorch to actually exercise the GPU. Together, these properties mean the 180-run pilot matrix is a one-time evaluation with a fully auditable trail rather than a result that depended on unrecorded randomness or an unpinned, silently-drifting dependency set, even though, consistent with the limitation already noted above, each (condition, file) pair was itself only run once.

# 6. Results and Analysis

## 6.1 Overview of the Pilot Run

The pilot evaluation comprised 180 transcription runs (36 samples × 5 conditions), all of which completed without a runtime error or an invalid metric value. Table 6.1 summarises mean WER, CER, and RTF for each condition across all 36 samples.

**Table 6.1 — Aggregate results by condition (n=36 per condition)**

| Condition | Model | Mean WER | Mean CER | Mean RTF |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A — Baseline | tiny | 63.4% | 43.6% | 0.323 |
| B — Noise reduction | tiny | 62.2% | 44.6% | 0.372 |
| D — Domain prompt | tiny | 61.3% | 45.2% | 0.351 |
| E — Forced language | tiny | 59.6% | 41.1% | 0.260 |
| F — Base model | base | 48.3% | 33.6% | 0.635 |

Two things are visible immediately and shape the rest of this chapter. First, absolute WER is high, between roughly 48% and 63%, by the standards of headline Whisper benchmarks. This is expected rather than alarming: the dataset is real 8kHz telephone-quality spontaneous speech rather than studio-quality read speech, the models tested are the two smallest available (`tiny` and `base`), and one of the two conditions per sample is deliberately degraded with added noise. Manual inspection of individual transcripts (e.g. "bank" recognised as "bake", German "einzuzahlen" recognised as "einzuzeilen") confirms these are genuine, explainable acoustic-confusion errors rather than pipeline failures, consistent with zero runs producing an error or an undefined metric. Second, the five conditions move WER by a relatively modest amount, except for the model-size change in Condition F, which is the largest single effect observed in the entire pilot. The remainder of this chapter unpacks each condition against the research questions and hypotheses set out in Sections 1.4 and 5.4.

**Table 6.2 — Mean WER and CER by noise condition (n=24 clean / 12 noisy per condition)**

| Condition | Clean WER | Clean CER | Noisy WER | Noisy CER |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A — Baseline | 47.0% | 29.7% | 96.4% | 71.3% |
| B — Noise reduction | 49.2% | 32.4% | 88.1% | 68.8% |
| D — Domain prompt | 49.5% | 33.3% | 84.9% | 69.1% |
| E — Forced language | 47.3% | 30.9% | 84.1% | 61.3% |
| F — Base model | 32.9% | 22.3% | 79.2% | 56.2% |

Table 6.2 breaks down mean WER and CER by noise condition for each of the five conditions, extending the clean/noisy split discussed qualitatively in Sections 6.2 and 6.5 to all five conditions at once. Two patterns are visible across the full table that are not obvious from the aggregate numbers in Table 6.1 alone. First, noisy-audio WER falls steadily across the chained conditions A through E (96.4%, 88.1%, 84.9%, 84.1%), a cumulative improvement of 12.3 points from baseline to forced language, even though each individual intervention's isolated effect, examined separately in Sections 6.2 through 6.4, is comparatively modest; the chained design described in Section 5.3 is precisely what allows this cumulative pattern to be attributed to specific, named causes rather than observed only as an unexplained aggregate trend. Second, clean-audio WER does not fall monotonically across the same chain (47.0%, 49.2%, 49.5%, 47.3%): both noise reduction and domain prompting carry a small clean-audio cost that forced language only partially offsets, which is consistent with the partial-support verdicts already reached for H1 and H2 in Section 6.6.

## 6.2 RQ1 / H1 — Noise and the Effect of Noise-Reduction Preprocessing

Splitting Condition A by noise type shows that background noise is, by a wide margin, the single largest driver of error observed in this pilot: WER roughly doubles from clean to noisy audio under the baseline configuration (47.0% clean vs. 96.4% noisy), and a similar near-doubling holds across every other condition tested. No other manipulation in this study, preprocessing, prompting, language forcing, or model size, comes close to this magnitude of effect. This directly supports framing noise robustness as the central practical challenge for ASR in corporate meeting settings, ahead of either domain vocabulary or model selection.

Comparing Condition B against Condition A isolates the effect of `noisereduce`-based preprocessing. On noisy audio, preprocessing reduces WER from 96.4% to 88.1%, an 8.3-point improvement. On clean audio, however, preprocessing slightly worsens WER, from 47.0% to 49.2%, a 2.2-point regression. The net effect across the full, noise-mixed sample is a small overall improvement (63.4% to 62.2% mean WER), because the gain on noisy clips outweighs the small loss on clean ones. This is a partial confirmation of H1: noise reduction does help on noisy audio as hypothesised, but it is not free on clean audio as H1 had assumed, which is consistent with how spectral-gating denoising methods are known to behave, trading a small amount of fidelity on an already-clean signal in exchange for resilience to actual noise. For a deployment that cannot predict in advance whether a given meeting will be clean or noisy, this trade-off, and not just the net average, is the more decision-relevant finding.

## 6.3 RQ2 / H2 — Domain Vocabulary Prompting

Comparing Condition D against Condition B isolates the effect of injecting a fixed Nestlé-vocabulary prompt into the decoder. The net effect on aggregate WER is small, 62.2% to 61.3%, a 1.1-point improvement, with mean CER moving in the opposite direction (44.6% to 45.2%). This is best read as a null result rather than as either a confirmation or a refutation of H2, for a specific and previously identified reason: the evaluation dataset, built from PolyAI/MInDS-14 banking-intent speech (Section 5.1), contains none of the Nestlé brand or executive vocabulary the prompt is designed to help recognise. Condition D can therefore only show whether domain prompting damages general transcription accuracy when the prompted vocabulary is absent from the audio, which it does not appear to do, and cannot show whether it helps when that vocabulary is actually present, which would require Nestlé-domain audio outside the scope of what this thesis could use.

This dataset constraint is precisely what motivated treating domain-name recognition as a problem worth studying in the first place. During early, informal prototyping of this pipeline, ahead of any formal evaluation, a colleague's name, Philippe Navratil, was observed on at least one occasion to be transcribed as "Philippe Naruto." This observation is reported here once, explicitly as an anecdotal, single, informally observed example rather than as measured data, since it was never captured under controlled conditions and should not be confused with a pilot result. It is nonetheless consistent with a failure mode the pilot evaluation did capture under controlled conditions, in the opposite direction: on one noisy German clip (`minds14_de_noisy_02.wav`), whose reference transcript has nothing to do with Nestlé, the model hallucinated the prompted vocabulary directly into its output, producing the phrase "...it's Nestlé Ghana, the center of the conference room..." This is a concrete, reproducible example of prompt leakage, where a low-confidence decoding pass (here, noisy audio combined with automatic language detection) appears to fall back on text echoed from the initial prompt rather than the actual acoustic content. Read together, the informal Navratil anecdote and this measured hallucination point in the same direction: static prompt injection is a double-edged mitigation, capable of biasing the model toward organisation-specific names but also capable of injecting those same names where they do not belong, particularly when the model's confidence is already low for other reasons. A dataset that actually contained Nestlé vocabulary in its reference transcripts would be needed to measure the benefit side of this trade-off with the same rigour this pilot was able to apply to the cost side.

## 6.4 RQ3 / H4 — Reliability of Automatic Language Identification

Comparing Condition E (forced language) against Condition D (automatic detection) isolates the effect of language identification specifically. The aggregate effect is a 1.7-point WER improvement (61.3% to 59.6%) and a larger 4.1-point CER improvement (45.2% to 41.1%), but the aggregate hides a sharply uneven, language-specific pattern that is the strongest and most clearly attributable finding of this entire pilot. Comparing hypothesis text directly between the auto-detect and forced-language runs on the same 12 audio files per language shows that English transcripts were identical in all 12 cases, a 0% mismatch rate; German transcripts differed in 2 of 12 cases (about 17%); and French transcripts differed in 5 of 12 cases (about 42%). This had to be established by directly comparing hypothesis text between runs, because, as noted in Section 4.3, the evaluation harness does not log Whisper's own detected language per run, only the language that was requested.

The French mismatches are not minor wording differences; several involve catastrophic script-level failures. One French reference transcript, "bonjour je voudrais changer d'adresse," was auto-detected and transcribed as "Боже, будь, дай, дай, дай, дай," Cyrillic text with no relationship to the spoken content; other short, noisy French clips were similarly mis-transcribed into Hebrew-, Turkish-, or Chinese-script text under automatic detection. Forcing the language parameter to `fr` recovered French-script output, at least partially correct, in every one of these cases. Notably, this script-level recovery is only partially reflected in the per-language WER breakdown: aggregate French WER is nearly unchanged between Condition D and Condition E (74.8% in both), while the improvement from forced language is concentrated in German (48.9% to 43.9%); English shows no change in either WER or transcript content, consistent with the 0% mismatch rate already reported. The likely explanation is that word-level WER scores a wrong-script hallucination and a same-script-but-still-erroneous transcript similarly poorly when neither matches the reference closely, even though the latter is a qualitatively far more usable failure mode for a human reader. Read as a whole, this is a strong, specific, and reproducible argument that automatic language identification should not be relied upon for short or noisy utterances in a genuinely multilingual deployment, and that forcing the expected language per speaker, channel, or meeting is a low-cost mitigation with a clearly demonstrated benefit, independent of whether it moves a single aggregate WER number.

**Table 6.3 — Mean WER by language (n=12 per language per condition)**

| Condition | English | French | German |
|---|---|---|---|
| A — Baseline | 69.6% | 69.1% | 51.6% |
| B — Noise reduction | 64.3% | 73.3% | 48.9% |
| D — Domain prompt | 60.2% | 74.8% | 48.9% |
| E — Forced language | 60.2% | 74.8% | 43.9% |
| F — Base model | 47.7% | 64.1% | 33.2% |

Table 6.3 reports the same per-language breakdown for all five conditions, rather than only the D-versus-E comparison discussed above, which makes it possible to check whether the language-specific pattern already identified is specific to that one pair or holds across the whole chain. German has the lowest mean WER of the three languages in every one of the five conditions, by a substantial margin (13 to 31 points below the next-best language depending on condition), while French has the highest WER in four of the five conditions, the exception being Condition A, where it is marginally below English's 69.6%. This thesis does not have a verified causal explanation for the German advantage: it could reflect genuine cross-linguistic differences in how well Whisper's training data covers German telephone speech specifically, or it could equally reflect sample-specific characteristics of the particular German clips drawn from PolyAI/MInDS-14, such as speaker clarity or recording quality, that have nothing to do with language as such. With twelve clips per language this pilot cannot distinguish between those explanations, and Table 6.3 is reported here as a descriptive finding rather than as evidence for either one; resolving it would require a larger, language-balanced sample, which is noted as a direction for future work in Section 8.4.

## 6.5 RQ4 / H3 — Model Size, Accuracy, and Real-Time Factor

Comparing Condition F (`base` model) against Condition B (`tiny` model, otherwise identical configuration) isolates the effect of model size. Mean WER falls from 62.2% to 48.3%, a 13.9-point improvement, and mean CER falls from 44.6% to 33.6%. The improvement holds consistently across both noise conditions (clean: 49.2% to 32.9%; noisy: 88.1% to 79.2%) and across all three languages tested, indicating a broad accuracy gain rather than one driven by a single language or condition. This comes at a real but bounded computational cost: mean RTF increases from 0.372 to 0.635, the base model takes roughly 1.9 times longer to process the same audio, but remains comfortably below the RTF = 1.0 real-time threshold. Across the full pilot, RTF was below 1.0 on 169 of 180 runs (93.9%), with the `tiny` configurations averaging RTF ≈ 0.326 and the `base` configuration averaging RTF ≈ 0.635, all measured on CPU-only hardware with no GPU acceleration. This supports H3 (a CPU-only deployment can run faster than real time) for both model sizes tested in this pilot, and demonstrates a genuine, measured accuracy/speed trade-off point as model size increases. Whether this trend continues favourably at the larger `small`, `medium`, or `large-v2` Whisper sizes is a reasonable extrapolation given the consistent direction of the effect, but it was not measured directly in this thesis, since downloading and running those larger checkpoints was judged impractical within the project's CPU-only, no-GPU sandbox.

Two further details from the per-file results table (reproduced in full in Appendix D) sharpen this picture beyond the aggregate RTF figures above. First, model size can occasionally resolve a difficult file almost completely: `minds14_en_clean_04.wav`, which Condition A transcribes with 71.43% WER, is transcribed by Condition F with 0.00% WER, a perfect match against the reference transcript, the single largest per-file improvement attributable to model size anywhere in the pilot. Second, the eleven runs (of 180) whose RTF exceeded the real-time threshold of 1.0 are concentrated in a specific, identifiable pattern rather than scattered randomly: eight of the eleven occur under Condition F, predominantly on noisy clips, and the single slowest run in the entire pilot, `minds14_fr_noisy_04.wav` under Condition F, took 10.3 seconds to process and reached an RTF of 2.877, nearly three times slower than real time. The remaining three slow runs occur on the same two noisy French files (`minds14_fr_noisy_02.wav` and `minds14_fr_noisy_04.wav`) under the `tiny` model with preprocessing and prompting enabled, which suggests these two specific clips, rather than the noisy condition or the French language in general, are disproportionately expensive to decode regardless of model size, plausibly because Whisper's decoder repeatedly extends a long, low-confidence hypothesis on audio it is struggling to transcribe rather than terminating early, a pattern consistent with the hallucination behaviour discussed in Sections 6.3 and 6.4. Breaking the model-size comparison down by language confirms the gain from Condition B to Condition F is broad rather than concentrated in one language: WER falls by 16.6 points for English (64.3% to 47.7%), 9.2 points for French (73.3% to 64.1%), and 15.7 points for German (48.9% to 33.2%).

## 6.6 Discussion: Hypotheses Revisited

H1 (noise reduction helps noisy audio without materially hurting clean audio) is partially supported: it helps noisy audio as predicted, but does measurably cost a small amount of accuracy on clean audio, a nuance the original hypothesis did not anticipate. H2 (domain prompting reduces WER on named entities) is neither confirmed nor refuted by this pilot; the evaluation dataset's lack of Nestlé-domain content means the relevant comparison could not be made, and the result obtained, a roughly neutral effect on general transcription plus one observed instance of prompt-leakage hallucination, answers a different, narrower question than the one H2 originally posed. H3 (RTF below 1.0 across tested model sizes on CPU hardware) is supported, with the caveat that it was only tested for `tiny` and `base`, not for larger Whisper variants. H4 (forced language outperforms automatic detection, particularly on short or noisy clips) is strongly supported, and is the most robust, clearly attributable finding to come out of this pilot: the English/German/French mismatch gradient (0%, 17%, 42%) and the qualitative severity of the French failures make a specific, actionable case for language forcing in any genuinely multilingual deployment of this pipeline, independent of how it is reflected in any single aggregate accuracy number.

Two limitations bound how far these results can be generalised, beyond those already noted for each condition individually. First, the dataset is a pilot-scale sample of 36 clips, twelve per language, drawn from a single substitute corpus; this is sufficient to establish the directional findings reported above but not to support tight statistical confidence intervals or claims of significance in a formal sense. Second, each (condition, file) pair was run once rather than repeated, so RTF in particular should be read as indicative of typical throughput on this sandbox's CPU rather than as a precise laboratory measurement. Both limitations, together with the Condition C/VAD and language-logging gaps already discussed in Sections 4.3 and 5.3, are carried forward into the future-work discussion in Chapter 8.

# 7. Integration and Deployment

## 7.1 Integration with OCR and Retrieval-Augmented Question Answering

As described in Section 4.1, the STT pipeline evaluated in this thesis is not a standalone tool but one of three content sources feeding a shared retrieval layer alongside a Topic 1 OCR pipeline for scanned documents. When `POST /transcribe` completes, the resulting transcript is embedded using a multilingual sentence-transformer model (`intfloat/multilingual-e5-small`, chosen specifically for its native coverage of English, French, and German) and stored in a ChromaDB collection alongside OCR-derived text from documents. The `POST /ask` endpoint then answers a natural-language question by retrieving the most relevant chunks across both sources and passing them, together with the question, to a locally hosted Ollama language model, which is instructed to answer only from the retrieved context and to cite the source filename and content type. From an accessibility standpoint, this is the more strategically important capability than transcription in isolation: it means a hearing-impaired employee can ask a question about something discussed in a meeting and receive an answer grounded in the actual transcript, rather than needing to read or search the raw transcript directly. This thesis evaluates the transcription quality that feeds into this layer (Chapters 5 and 6) but does not evaluate the retrieval or answer-generation quality of the `/ask` endpoint itself, which is outside its scope and belongs more properly to the Topic 3 component of the broader project.

The retrieval mechanics behind `/ask` are concrete and worth stating precisely, since the description above covers only which content sources feed the pipeline. `ingest()` (`backend/rag/vectorstore.py`) splits each incoming transcript or OCR page into word-based chunks of 500 words with a 50-word overlap between consecutive chunks, discarding any resulting chunk under 30 characters as too short to be useful context; each chunk is stored in a single, shared ChromaDB collection (`nestle_accessibility`) configured for cosine similarity, alongside metadata recording its source document, source type (`"stt"` or `"ocr"`), filename, and chunk index. `query()` retrieves the five most similar chunks to a given question (`n_results=5`, hardcoded at the call site in `backend/main.py`) using a single embedding-similarity pass, with no cross-encoder reranking step applied afterward to re-score or reorder that initial top-five list. This is a reasonable, low-latency design for a pilot-scale single-tenant deployment, but it means retrieval quality depends entirely on how well the multilingual e5-small embedding model's first-pass similarity ranking matches what a human would judge most relevant, an assumption this thesis does not separately test, since evaluating `/ask`'s retrieval or answer quality is explicitly outside its scope.

## 7.2 The Phase 1 / Phase 2 Migration Path

The platform's codebase consistently distinguishes between a "Phase 1" configuration, the one actually built, run, and evaluated in this thesis, and a "Phase 2" set of planned migrations to managed Azure services, documented as inline comments and, in one case, partially scaffolded code. Four concrete migration targets are identified in the codebase: replacing faster-whisper with Azure Cognitive Services Speech for transcription, replacing the local Tesseract OCR stub with Azure Document Intelligence (Form Recognizer), replacing the ChromaDB vector store with Azure AI Search, and replacing the locally hosted Ollama model with Azure OpenAI for answer generation. Of these, only the Azure OpenAI path has any code written against it, and that code is a commented-out client call guarded by a `NotImplementedError`, intended to be activated by setting an environment variable once Azure credentials are available; it has not been executed or tested as part of this thesis.

This stub pattern is not unique to the answer-generation path: reading the other three Phase 2 migration points directly shows a related but not identical structure. `backend/rag/vectorstore.py`, `backend/ocr/engine.py`, and `backend/stt/engine.py` each carry their own short header comment naming the intended Azure replacement, Azure AI Search, Azure Document Intelligence, and Azure Cognitive Services Speech respectively, but only the Azure OpenAI path in `backend/rag/chain.py` has an actual function stub (`_ask_azure_openai()`) with commented-out client-construction code behind it; the other three are documentation-only comments with no corresponding stub function. Where the stub does exist, the pattern is consistent and deliberately fails loudly rather than silently: setting the `LLM_PROVIDER` environment variable to `"azure_openai"` reaches `_ask_azure_openai()` through a simple dispatch check, but the function immediately raises `NotImplementedError` naming the missing configuration step, rather than falling through to a default provider or returning an empty or misleading answer. Consistent with this code never being exercised, `pyproject.toml` declares no Azure SDK dependency of any kind, no `azure-ai-*` package and no `openai` package either, since the commented-out `from openai import AzureOpenAI` import is never actually executed, and no test in the project's test suite references `_ask_azure_openai`, `LLM_PROVIDER`, or the `chain` module that contains it. The Phase 2 migration path described in this section is therefore accurately characterised as a named, deliberately-not-yet-implemented intention recorded directly in the code, rather than as a partially working feature that happens not to have been tested.

The evaluation tooling itself separately notes that all results in Chapter 6 were obtained on local, CPU-only hardware, and explicitly proposes re-running the same experimental matrix on a GPU-enabled Azure VM as a natural next step, in order to measure how the accuracy/RTF trade-off documented in Section 6.5 shifts under GPU acceleration. This thesis does not make that measurement, and does not claim a specific Azure VM size, service tier, or cost figure, since none was provisioned or tested; it records the migration path as it exists in the codebase's own documentation, and proposes the GPU comparison as future work rather than as a result already obtained.

## 7.3 Security, Privacy, and Deployment Considerations

Operating entirely on local, open-source components in its current Phase 1 form has one clear practical advantage for a project handling potentially sensitive meeting content: audio, transcripts, and embeddings never leave the machine running the backend, since there is no external API call in the transcription, embedding, or answer-generation path. This is a meaningful, if modest, privacy property, and it was a genuine design consideration behind choosing locally hosted alternatives (faster-whisper, Ollama, ChromaDB) over a cloud API at this stage of the project, rather than an accidental byproduct. That property would change under the Phase 2 migration discussed above, since routing audio or transcripts to a managed cloud STT or LLM service necessarily means that data leaves the local environment, which would need to be weighed explicitly against the latency, accuracy, and maintenance benefits such a migration could bring. At the same time, the current implementation does not include the access-control, authentication, or secrets-management layers that a genuine multi-user, enterprise-facing deployment would require: there is no user authentication on any endpoint, and no mechanism for restricting which uploaded files or transcripts a given user can later retrieve through `/ask`. This thesis treats these as known, explicitly out-of-scope gaps for a pilot-stage system rather than as solved problems, and does not claim a specific authentication, identity, or secrets-management solution was chosen or evaluated, since none was implemented or tested as part of this work.

One further security-relevant observation follows from the retrieval mechanics described in Section 7.1: because `/ask` performs a single embedding-similarity lookup with no reranking, authentication, or per-user scoping, any client able to reach the endpoint can retrieve chunks ingested from any previously uploaded file, OCR or STT, regardless of who uploaded it or what meeting it came from, which is a direct instance of the access-control gap already noted above rather than a separate issue. Equally, the project's pattern of failing loudly and explicitly, a raised `NotImplementedError` naming the missing configuration, rather than silently substituting a default or degraded behaviour, visible in the Azure OpenAI stub discussed in Section 7.2, is a defensible default for a pilot system: a deployment that mistakenly sets `LLM_PROVIDER=azure_openai` without configuring Azure credentials will fail immediately and visibly rather than serving incorrect or empty answers without warning.

# 8. Conclusion and Future Work

## 8.1 Summary of Findings

This thesis set out to measure, rather than assume, how a CPU-only, open-source Whisper-based STT pipeline performs on noisy, multilingual speech, and how far simple, low-cost mitigations close the resulting gap. Across a 180-run pilot evaluation on a real, multilingual, partly noised dataset, four findings stand out. Background noise is by far the dominant source of error, roughly doubling WER relative to clean audio across every condition tested, well ahead of any other factor studied. Lightweight noise-reduction preprocessing measurably helps on noisy audio but carries a small accuracy cost on clean audio, making it a genuine trade-off rather than an unambiguous win. Domain-vocabulary prompting could not be shown to help recognise the vocabulary it targets, given a dataset that does not contain that vocabulary, but it was shown, concretely, to be capable of leaking the prompted text into unrelated, low-confidence transcriptions, an outcome consistent with an earlier, informally observed naming error during prototyping. Automatic language identification is unreliable for short or noisy non-English audio in this pipeline, failing in close to half of the French clips tested and occasionally producing wrong-script, unusable output, while forcing the correct language per utterance reliably recovers usable transcription; this is the most robust and actionable finding of the pilot. Finally, moving from the smallest to the next-smallest Whisper model size yields a substantial accuracy improvement at a bounded, still-real-time computational cost on CPU-only hardware.

Two further patterns from the per-condition and per-file breakdowns in Chapter 6 refine this picture. The cumulative WER improvement on noisy audio strengthens steadily across the chained conditions A through E, even though no single intervention in that chain produces a large effect on its own, which illustrates that several modest, individually inconclusive mitigations can compound into a more meaningful aggregate gain. German speech consistently showed the lowest WER of the three languages tested across every condition, by a wide and consistent margin, a pattern this thesis can report but, with only twelve clips per language, cannot explain with confidence; it is reported as an open, unresolved observation rather than a finding with a settled cause.

## 8.2 Contributions Revisited

Returning to the four contributions stated in Section 1.5, this thesis has delivered a small, licensed, multilingual evaluation dataset built from a real speech corpus after the originally planned alternatives proved impractical, with that substitution and its consequences reported rather than concealed; a 180-run, zero-error pilot evaluation with WER, CER, and RTF computed via a transparent, self-contained scoring implementation; an explicit, documented account of two implementation gaps, unwired VAD and unlogged detected language, discovered during the evaluation itself rather than assumed away; and a results chapter built entirely from measured pilot data, replacing the single recycled performance claim and repeated anecdote that characterised earlier project documentation with varied, source-grounded findings, including findings that only partially or did not confirm the hypotheses that motivated them.

## 8.3 Limitations

The principal limitations of this work, several already noted at the point they first arise, are gathered here. The evaluation dataset, at 36 clips across three languages, is pilot-scale and does not support strong statistical claims; it is also drawn from telephone-banking speech rather than corporate meeting audio, and contains no Nestlé-specific vocabulary, which directly limits what the domain-prompting condition could demonstrate. The "noisy" condition is synthetic white noise at a fixed signal-to-noise ratio, not a field recording of an actual noisy meeting room, and so does not capture babble noise, reverberation, or codec artefacts. Voice-activity-detection preprocessing, part of the original experimental design, was not evaluated because it is not wired into the pipeline's transcription path, a gap discovered rather than assumed during this work. Speaker diarisation, while implemented in the codebase, was not exercised in the pilot, both for lack of a configured access token and because the dataset's largely single-speaker utterances would not have produced a meaningful diarisation-accuracy measurement. Each condition was run once per file rather than repeated, so reported RTF values are indicative rather than laboratory-precise. Finally, all results were obtained on CPU-only sandbox hardware with no GPU acceleration, and do not characterise performance on the GPU-backed infrastructure a production deployment, Azure-hosted or otherwise, would likely use.

The retrieval layer underlying the `/ask` endpoint (Section 7.1) was not evaluated for relevance or answer quality in this thesis, and its single-pass, unreranked retrieval design has not been tested for whether it would surface a meaningful share of irrelevant chunks at larger document volumes than the small pilot corpus actually ingested. The same endpoint lacks any per-user or per-meeting access scoping, so any client able to reach it can retrieve content from any previously ingested transcript or document, a gap noted in Section 7.3 as a deployment blocker rather than a research finding, but listed here as well because it bears directly on whether this pipeline, in its current form, could be exposed to real end users.

## 8.4 Future Work

Several concrete, scoped extensions follow directly from the limitations above, rather than from speculative redesign. Wiring the existing Silero VAD loader into the actual transcription path would complete the originally planned experimental matrix and allow Condition C to be measured rather than reported as absent. Logging Whisper's detected language per run in the evaluation harness would turn the indirect, diff-based language-mismatch measurement in Section 6.4 into a direct one. Constructing or obtaining a small, properly licensed evaluation set that actually contains Nestlé-relevant vocabulary, even a few dozen utterances, would allow the benefit side of domain-vocabulary prompting to finally be measured, rather than only its cost, as this pilot was able to do. Repeating the experimental matrix on a GPU-enabled machine, as the project's own evaluation tooling already proposes, would extend the model-size/RTF trade-off curve established in Section 6.5 to larger Whisper checkpoints and clarify whether the accuracy gains observed from `tiny` to `base` continue at the same rate. Finally, before any version of this pipeline is exposed to real users beyond a pilot setting, the authentication, access-control, and data-governance gaps noted in Section 7.3 would need to be addressed directly, regardless of whether the system remains local or migrates toward the Phase 2 Azure path documented in the codebase.

A further, narrower extension follows directly from the multilingual embedding model already in use: because `intfloat/multilingual-e5-small` embeds English, French, and German text into a shared vector space, the retrieval layer is in principle already capable of cross-lingual retrieval, surfacing a French-language transcript chunk in response to an English-language question, or vice versa, without any additional configuration. Whether it actually does so with useful accuracy was not measured in this thesis and would be a natural, scoped follow-up evaluation, particularly relevant for a genuinely multilingual corporate deployment where a question asked in one language may need to be answered from a meeting conducted in another. Adding a lightweight cross-encoder reranking step on top of the existing five-chunk retrieval, and introducing per-user or per-meeting access scoping on `/ask`, are two further, concretely scoped engineering extensions that follow from the retrieval-layer observations in Sections 7.1 and 7.3.

**Domain-specific fine-tuning rather than prompting alone.** The original project brief envisaged transfer learning and domain-specific fine-tuning as a route to higher accuracy on organisation-specific vocabulary; this thesis deliberately limited itself to prompt-based domain adaptation, since fine-tuning a Whisper or wav2vec 2.0 checkpoint was impractical within the project's CPU-only, no-GPU sandbox and, more fundamentally, no labelled in-domain Nestlé corpus was available. Two recent directions are nonetheless worth recording as the natural next step, because both specifically target the data-scarcity constraint that blocked this work. Text-only fine-tuning adapts a speech-language-model ASR system to a target domain using only target-domain text, with no labelled in-domain audio at all (Fang et al., 2025); this is directly applicable to the Nestlé setting, where meeting recordings are confidential but written minutes, reports, and brand glossaries are readily available. A complementary decoupled two-stage approach first applies parameter-efficient LoRA fine-tuning of the language component on target-domain text, then adapts the acoustic encoder on unlabelled in-domain audio via pseudo-labelling, reporting a 19.2% relative word-error-rate reduction without any labelled in-domain audio pairs (Zheng et al., 2026). Both are parameter-efficient and could realistically be run on the GPU hardware noted below, making domain-adaptive fine-tuning the most concrete and highest-value extension of this work, and the one that most directly answers the adaptation question posed in the original research brief.

**GPU acceleration of the evaluation pipeline.** All results reported in Chapter 6 were produced on CPU-only hardware using faster-whisper's INT8 quantised code path, as stated in Section 5.5. To estimate how the accuracy/throughput trade-off documented in Section 6.5 would shift under GPU acceleration, the identical five-condition matrix was subsequently re-executed on a CUDA-enabled GPU using float16 precision. The real-time factor improved substantially and uniformly across every condition, while remaining well below 1.0 in the CPU case already:

| Condition | RTF (CPU, int8) | RTF (GPU, float16) | Speed-up |
|---|---|---|---|
| A — Baseline (tiny) | 0.323 | 0.143 | 2.3× |
| B — Noise reduction (tiny) | 0.372 | 0.144 | 2.6× |
| D — Domain prompt (tiny) | 0.351 | 0.144 | 2.4× |
| E — Forced language (tiny) | 0.260 | 0.138 | 1.9× |
| F — Base model (base) | 0.635 | 0.188 | 3.4× |

The gain was largest on the `base` model (3.4×), which is consistent with GPU acceleration paying off more on the heavier compute load. Two caveats bound this comparison. First, as in Chapter 6, each (condition, file) pair was run once, so these RTF values are indicative of typical throughput rather than precise laboratory measurements. Second, and more importantly, the WER and CER values are not reported here from the GPU run and must not be conflated with the canonical Chapter 6 figures: switching from INT8 to float16 changes the numerical representation of the model weights, which propagates through Whisper's autoregressive decoder and caused roughly 52 of the 180 transcriptions to differ slightly from their CPU counterparts. This means "deterministic", in the sense used in Section 5.5, holds for a fixed (hardware, compute-type) pairing but not across them. The numerical reproducibility of quantised ASR across compute backends is itself a question worth a dedicated, repeated-trial study, and is noted here as a direction rather than a result.

# Appendix A: Dataset Metadata Structure

The evaluation dataset described in Section 5.1 is indexed by a single CSV file, `datasets/stt_eval/metadata.csv`, with six columns: `audio_filename`, `reference_filename`, `language`, `noise_condition`, `speaker_count`, and `notes`. This is the complete schema; there are no nested fields, no JSON sidecar files, and no speaker-identity or accessibility metadata of any kind. `load_metadata()` and `validate_samples()` in `backend/evaluation/dataset.py` require five of these six columns to be present and non-empty (`notes` is informational only and is not validated).

The table below reproduces a representative sample of the 36 rows, one per language and noise condition, exactly as they appear in the file:

| audio_filename | reference_filename | language | noise_condition | speaker_count | notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| minds14_en_clean_01.wav | minds14_en_clean_01.txt | en | clean | 1 | PolyAI/minds14 (CC-BY-4.0), real telephone-banking speech |
| minds14_en_noisy_01.wav | minds14_en_clean_01.txt | en | noisy | 1 | Synthetic white noise added at 8dB SNR over PolyAI/minds14 source (same reference transcript) |
| minds14_fr_clean_01.wav | minds14_fr_clean_01.txt | fr | clean | 1 | PolyAI/minds14 (CC-BY-4.0), real telephone-banking speech |
| minds14_fr_noisy_01.wav | minds14_fr_clean_01.txt | fr | noisy | 1 | Synthetic white noise added at 8dB SNR over PolyAI/minds14 source (same reference transcript) |
| minds14_de_clean_01.wav | minds14_de_clean_01.txt | de | clean | 1 | PolyAI/minds14 (CC-BY-4.0), real telephone-banking speech |
| minds14_de_noisy_01.wav | minds14_de_clean_01.txt | de | noisy | 1 | Synthetic white noise added at 8dB SNR over PolyAI/minds14 source (same reference transcript) |

Three structural points are worth stating explicitly, because an earlier draft of this thesis described a materially different metadata system. First, every `noisy`-condition row shares its `reference_filename` with a `clean`-condition row from the same language: the noisy clips are not 12 independent recordings but synthetic 8dB-SNR white-noise variants generated from 12 of the 24 clean clips, so the dataset spans 24 distinct underlying utterances (8 per language), not 36. Second, `speaker_count` is 1 for all 36 rows without exception, because the underlying source material — PolyAI/MInDS-14 — consists of single-speaker telephone-banking utterances rather than multi-party meeting recordings; the column exists in the schema for forward compatibility with multi-speaker samples but carries no variation in the present dataset, a limitation already discussed in Section 5.4. Third, there is no speaker-identity field, no hearing-impairment or accessibility flag, and no enterprise identity-provider linkage (Azure Entra ID, Microsoft Graph, or otherwise) anywhere in this schema or in the code that consumes it. An earlier draft of this work described a JSON-schema metadata system with exactly these fields, modelled on an enterprise speaker-mapping and accessibility-tracking scheme; no such system was built, and the six-column CSV above is the complete, actual metadata structure used to produce every result reported in Chapter 6 and this appendix.

# Appendix B: API Documentation

This appendix documents the FastAPI backend's actual HTTP and WebSocket surface, as implemented in `backend/main.py` and described in Section 4.4. It supersedes an earlier draft of this appendix, which documented several endpoints, an authentication scheme, and a cloud storage layer that were never implemented; the discrepancies are listed at the end of this appendix rather than silently dropped, in keeping with the honesty standard applied throughout this rewrite.

**`POST /transcribe`** — Accepts a multipart file upload plus an optional `language` query parameter. Validates the uploaded filename's extension against an allow-list before proceeding; rejects anything outside it. Generates a `uuid4` document identifier, writes the file to `storage/uploads/`, runs it through the `faster-whisper`-based transcription pipeline described in Section 4.2 (using the optional `language` parameter to force a target language when supplied, otherwise auto-detecting), and ingests the resulting transcript into the ChromaDB collection via `rag.vectorstore.ingest()`. Returns a JSON object containing the document id, original filename, detected or forced language, full transcript text, a list of timestamped segments, audio duration, the Whisper model size in use, and the number of chunks ingested into the vector store.

**`WS /ws/transcribe`** — A WebSocket endpoint serving the live transcription path described in Section 4.3 (`RealTimeSTTHandler`). This is a separate code path from `/transcribe`, not a streaming wrapper around it: it hardcodes `language="fr"` rather than accepting a language parameter, uses a slightly different inline domain-prompt string than the batch path's `DEFAULT_DOMAIN_PROMPT`, and applies Silero VAD (0.5 threshold, 0.6s silence-end) and a trailing-10-second last-speaker-wins diarisation heuristic that differs from the batch path's temporal-overlap alignment. These differences are documented as an architectural inconsistency in Section 4.3, not as a deliberate design choice.

**`POST /ocr`** — Accepts an image or scanned-document upload and returns extracted text per page, ingesting the result into the same ChromaDB collection as `/transcribe` so that OCR'd and transcribed content are jointly retrievable through `/ask`.

**`POST /ask`** — Accepts a JSON body containing a natural-language question. Retrieves the top five most similar chunks (`n_results=5`, hardcoded, no reranking) from the shared ChromaDB collection via `rag.vectorstore.query()`, assembles them into a context window, and forwards the question and context to the configured LLM provider (dispatched via the `LLM_PROVIDER` environment variable, as described in Section 7.2). Returns the answer text together with the source chunks used, each tagged with its originating document and chunk index.

**`GET /health`** — Returns a JSON object with `status`, `ollama_available`, `whisper_model`, `llm_provider`, and `chroma_collection_count` fields. `ollama_available` is determined by a 3-second-timeout call to Ollama's own `/api/tags` endpoint; the route does not verify that the Whisper, VAD, or diarisation models referenced elsewhere in the response have finished loading, so a `200` response with `status: "ok"` does not guarantee the STT pipeline is actually ready to serve `/transcribe` requests. This is noted as a real gap in Section 4.4, not resolved in this rewrite.

**`GET /`** and **`GET /live`** — Serve the static HTML front ends for the batch-upload and live-transcription pages respectively, via `FileResponse`, with `include_in_schema=False` so neither appears in the auto-generated OpenAPI documentation. An unused `StaticFiles` import remains in `backend/main.py` from an earlier iteration of static-asset serving.

**Corrections to the original draft.** The original draft of this appendix documented a `/summarize` endpoint, a `/custom-vocabulary` endpoint, and a `/search` endpoint; none of the three exist in the codebase. It also documented OAuth2/Microsoft Entra ID bearer-token authentication on every route, Azure Blob Storage as the file-storage backend, Microsoft Graph API ingestion from Teams meeting recordings, Azure AI Search as a secondary retrieval index, Azure OpenAI as the sole answer-generation backend with built-in summarization, Azure Monitor-based request telemetry, and asynchronous batch job tracking with a polling endpoint. None of these exist either: the backend has no authentication on any route, stores files on local disk, has a single local LLM provider path with an unimplemented Azure OpenAI stub (Appendix C), and processes every request synchronously. The actual, complete endpoint surface is the seven routes listed above.

# Appendix C: Architecture — As Built and Planned Migration

**Phase 1, as built.** The system is a single FastAPI process (`backend/main.py`) with an async `lifespan` handler that pre-loads four models at startup: a `faster-whisper` ASR model sized by the `WHISPER_MODEL` environment variable (default `small`), a Silero VAD model, a `pyannote.audio` speaker-diarisation pipeline (gated behind the availability of a Hugging Face access token; the pipeline is skipped, not substituted, if no token is configured), and a ChromaDB persistent client backing a single collection, `nestle_accessibility`, using `intfloat/multilingual-e5-small` embeddings and cosine similarity. CORS is configured permissively, a state appropriate to a local pilot but explicitly flagged in Section 7.3 as unsuitable for any deployment beyond it. There is no message queue, no separate worker process, no reverse proxy, and no container orchestration; the entire pipeline runs in-process behind Uvicorn.

**Phase 2, planned migration.** Section 7.2 identifies four components of this pipeline that a production deployment would plausibly migrate to managed Azure services: Azure Cognitive Services Speech in place of local `faster-whisper`, Azure AI Document Intelligence in place of the local OCR path, Azure AI Search in place of the local ChromaDB collection, and Azure OpenAI in place of the local LLM provider for `/ask`. As of this writing, three of these four exist only as code comments in the relevant modules, marking where the integration would go. The fourth, Azure OpenAI, has a real but non-functional stub: `_ask_azure_openai()` in `backend/rag/chain.py`, reachable through the same `LLM_PROVIDER` environment-variable dispatch used by the working local providers, but its body raises `NotImplementedError` rather than calling any Azure endpoint. There is no Azure SDK dependency declared in `pyproject.toml`, and no test in the project's test suite exercises this function. This stub represents the full extent of Phase 2 implementation at the time of writing.

**Correction to the original draft.** The original draft of this appendix described a fully built Azure Kubernetes Service deployment: containerised microservices behind AKS, Azure Blob Storage for audio and document persistence, Azure AI Search as the production retrieval index, Azure OpenAI as the production answer-generation backend, Microsoft Entra ID for authentication, Azure Key Vault for secrets management, and Azure Monitor and Microsoft Sentinel for telemetry and security monitoring, with a note that the diagram was "not reproduced here due to format constraints." No such deployment exists, was provisioned, or was tested at any point during this project. The honest Phase 2 status is the single `NotImplementedError` stub described above, alongside three unimplemented code-comment markers; everything else in the original Appendix C was invented.

# Appendix E: Domain Vocabulary Prompting

Section 4.2 and Table 5.2 describe the domain-prompt condition (D_domain_prompt) tested in this pilot: a fixed string, `DEFAULT_DOMAIN_PROMPT`, passed to faster-whisper's `initial_prompt` parameter, listing corporate and brand terms including "Nestlé," "Nespresso," "Maggi," "Purina," "KitKat," "Nescafé," and "Nestlé Ghana." The live WebSocket path (`/ws/transcribe`) uses a separate, slightly different inline string containing the same core brand list but omitting "Nestlé Ghana" — an inconsistency documented as a real architectural gap in Section 4.3, not as a deliberate experimental variable.

The actual, measured effect of this prompt on this dataset, reported in full in Section 6.3, was negative: because the 36 evaluation samples contain no Nestlé-related content (they are general-purpose telephone-banking utterances drawn from PolyAI/MInDS-14), the domain prompt's vocabulary had nothing relevant to anchor to, and instead biased decoding toward prompt-list terms. The clearest documented instance is a hypothesis transcript in which Whisper inserted the phrase "Nestlé Ghana" into output text where the corresponding reference transcript contains no such term — a direct, file-level example of prompt-induced hallucination, traceable to a specific row in `results/stt_eval_pilot_results.csv` under the D_domain_prompt condition tag. This is presented as the real, primary finding on domain-vocabulary prompting in this thesis: on a domain-mismatched dataset, the technique measurably hurt rather than helped.

This finding necessarily replaces an earlier draft's treatment of this topic, which built an extended narrative around invented example terms — "Nescafé Gold," "Maggi Cube," "Milo Activ-Go," a "Maggi Fortification" initiative, and a "Projet Milo" — and an anecdote in which the system was claimed to have corrected a mistranscription of a named individual's surname, "Philippe Navratil," from an ASR error rendering it as "Philippe Naruto," with the claim qualified as "(experiment in progress; results to be reported)." No such individual, recording, or experiment exists in this project's actual materials; the anecdote is not retained in any form here, including as a qualified or hedged claim, because no evidence for it exists. The honest, supportable finding — that domain-vocabulary prompting hurt accuracy on a domain-mismatched evaluation set, with one concrete hallucination example traceable to raw results data — is presented in its place. Constructing or obtaining a small evaluation set that actually contains Nestlé-relevant vocabulary, so that the benefit side of domain-prompting (rather than only its cost) could finally be measured, is listed as future work in Section 8.4.
